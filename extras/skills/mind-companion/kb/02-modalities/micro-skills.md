## What separates a clinical reply from an advice reply

The single largest difference between a trained clinician and a well-meaning helper is the ratio of understanding to instruction. A helper hears a problem and reaches for a solution. A clinician hears a problem and first establishes that they have understood it accurately, out loud, in a way the person can correct.

This is not politeness. It has three functions. It checks the clinician's model against the person's actual experience before anything is built on it. It gives the person the experience of being understood, which is itself associated with outcome: therapist empathy has a mean weighted r of .28 with outcome across 82 samples and 6,138 clients (Elliott, Bohart, Watson and Murphy, 2018). And it keeps the person doing the work, which matters because arguments for change are more durable when the person makes them.

Four failure patterns to recognise in one's own output:

Premature solution. Offering a strategy before the problem has been described in enough detail for the strategy to be sound.

Reassurance. Contradicting the person's distress with an optimistic claim. This reliably ends the disclosure, because the person now knows that this content produces reassurance rather than engagement.

Normalising too early. Saying "that's completely normal" before the person has finished saying what it is. The same sentence is useful thirty seconds later.

Question stacking. Two or three questions in a row with no reflection between them, which converts the conversation into an interview and shifts responsibility for direction entirely onto the clinician.

> Client: "I've been snapping at my kids all week and I hate myself for it."
> Advice reply: "That sounds like burnout. Have you tried scheduling some time for yourself? Even twenty minutes can help. And it might be worth looking at your sleep."
> Clinical reply: "Snapping at them, and then the hating yourself on top of it. Which of those is worse to sit with?"

> Client: "I don't think I can do this anymore."
> Advice reply: "It's important to remember how far you've come. Let's look at what's worked before."
> Clinical reply: "Can't do this anymore. I want to make sure I hear which 'this' you mean, because that could be a few different things."

Note that the clinical replies are shorter than the advice replies. This is generally true. Length in a helper's turn is usually a symptom of the helper doing the work.

## Simple reflection

A simple reflection restates what the person said, adding little or nothing. It stays within the content already offered. Its purposes are to signal accurate hearing, to slow the pace, and to keep the person talking without steering.

Forms: a near-repetition of key words; a paraphrase using the person's own vocabulary; a short partial reflection that picks up the loaded phrase and leaves it open.

> Client: "By the time I get home I've got nothing left."
> Simple reflection: "Nothing left by the time you get in."

> Client: "I keep thinking I should be over this by now."
> Simple reflection: "Should be over it."

The most powerful and least used form is the single phrase repeated back with the emphasis the person gave it, followed by silence. It invites elaboration without asking for it.

Rules. Do not add interpretation. Do not add a question on the end, because appending a question converts the reflection into a prompt and the person answers the question instead of continuing. Do not upgrade the emotion word. If they said irritated, do not reflect furious.

The most common error is the reflection formula. Beginning every reflection with "It sounds like" becomes audible as a tic within four or five uses and reads as technique rather than attention. Vary the openings, and often use none at all: a reflection can be a bare statement.

> Formulaic: "It sounds like you're feeling frustrated. It sounds like work has been really hard."
> Varied: "Frustrated." / "Work's been grinding you down." / "So that's two months of it now."

A reflection is a statement, not a question, and this shows in the intonation. Reflections delivered with a rising inflection function as questions and produce a yes or no rather than elaboration. In text, this means avoiding the question mark.

> Rising: "You're feeling like you can't cope?"
> Level: "You're at the point where coping isn't the word for it anymore."

## Complex reflection: continuing the paragraph, amplified and double-sided

A complex reflection adds meaning that was implied but not said. It is a guess, offered as a statement, that the person can accept or correct. Complex reflections do more work than simple ones and carry more risk of being wrong, and being wrong is not a problem provided the reflection is offered in a way that invites correction.

Continuing the paragraph. The clinician says the next sentence the person was heading toward but did not say.

> Client: "I told my sister I'd help with Mum, and I said yes, and then I got off the phone and just sat there."
> Continuing the paragraph: "And part of you already knew you'd regret saying yes."

Amplified reflection. The clinician reflects the statement slightly stronger than it was given, without sarcasm, which typically prompts the person to argue the other side themselves. This is the single most useful move when someone is defending a position, and it fails badly if there is any irony in the delivery.

> Client: "The drinking isn't really an issue. Everyone in my job drinks like that."
> Amplified: "So there's really nothing about it that concerns you."
> Client: "Well, I mean, I wouldn't say nothing. The mornings are getting harder."

Double-sided reflection. The clinician holds both sides of an ambivalence in one sentence, with the change side last, since whatever comes last tends to be what the person picks up.

> Client: "I know I should leave but I've been there eleven years and I'd be starting from zero."
> Double-sided: "Eleven years is a lot to walk away from, and you're the one who used the word 'should'."

Metaphor. Offering an image that captures the structure of the experience.

> Client: "Every time I think I'm getting somewhere something knocks it back."
> Metaphor: "Like you keep getting to the same point on the hill and then finding yourself at the bottom again."

Reframing. Reflecting the same content with a different meaning attached. Reframes should be offered lightly, because a reframe the person does not accept lands as correction.

> Client: "I'm so needy with him. I check my phone constantly."
> Reframe: "Or the checking is what happens when someone matters to you and you can't tell where you stand."

Calibration rule: complex reflections should be roughly one step ahead of the person, not three. A reflection that names a core belief in session two, when the person is describing a bad week, is not depth, it is a jump the person cannot use.

## Reflection of feeling and reflection of meaning

Reflection of feeling names the emotion, including emotion that was shown rather than stated. It is more exposing than content reflection and should be paced accordingly.

> Client: "I found the letters when I was clearing the house. There were about forty of them." (voice tightens)
> Content reflection: "Forty letters, in among her things."
> Feeling reflection: "Something happened just then when you said forty."

Accuracy matters more than intensity. An emotion word that is slightly off invites correction, and correction is useful. An emotion word that is much too strong makes the person defend against it.

> Too strong: "You must have been devastated."
> Calibrated: "There's some grief in that, and something else I can't name."

Reflection of meaning goes beneath the emotion to what the situation signifies for the person: what it says about them, what it threatens, what it costs.

> Client: "He didn't even ask how the scan went. He just talked about the car."
> Feeling: "That stung."
> Meaning: "It's not really about the car. It's that you're going through something enormous on your own, in a house with another person in it."

The order matters. Content, then feeling, then meaning is the usual depth ladder, and skipping to meaning too early produces the response "I suppose so" delivered flatly, which is the marker that the clinician has gone deeper than the person is currently standing.

When the person's stated feeling and shown feeling differ, name the discrepancy without asserting which is true.

> "You're telling me it's fine, and you're saying it with your jaw set. I don't know what to make of that, so I'm asking you."

## Summarising

Summaries do work that reflections cannot. They gather scattered material into a shape, demonstrate sustained attention across time, and hand the person a version of their own account they can revise.

Three types.

Collecting summaries gather several related items and end with an invitation to continue. They are short and used mid-conversation.

> "So far there's the sleep, the money, and the thing with your brother that you touched on and moved past. What else is in the pile?"

Linking summaries connect present material to something said earlier, often weeks earlier. These carry disproportionate weight because they show the clinician holds continuity.

> "What you just said about not wanting to bother the GP sounds like the same thing you described in January about not calling your daughter when you were ill. Both times, needing something felt like an imposition."

Transitional summaries close one section and open another, and are the standard way to move between agenda items or to end a session.

> "Let me pull together where we've got to. You came in wanting to work out whether to stay in the job. What's emerged is that the job is bearable and the commute isn't, and that underneath both is the sense you've given up on the thing you actually trained for. Have I got that right, or have I flattened something?"

The closing question is not optional. A summary offered without an invitation to correct is a pronouncement. Useful forms: "What did I miss?", "Where's that wrong?", "What would you add?"

In motivational interviewing, summaries are also used strategically. A summary that includes the person's change talk and their sustain talk, ordered so that change talk lands last, followed by "so where does that leave you?", is a standard move at the point of decision.

> "You've said the fatigue is affecting your work, that your daughter has noticed, and that you don't like who you are on the bad days. You've also said you don't want to be someone who can't have a drink at a wedding, and that you've stopped before and it didn't stick. And you've said the mornings are getting harder and you're tired of managing it. Where does that leave you?"

## Open questions and the question-to-reflection ratio

Open questions invite elaboration and cannot be answered with a word. Closed questions gather specific data. Both are necessary and the error is proportion, not the existence of closed questions.

A useful working target from motivational interviewing training is at least two reflections for every question, and a majority of questions open. This is a training heuristic rather than an outcome-validated threshold [UNVERIFIED as a precise standard], but as a self-monitoring device it reliably catches the drift into interrogation.

Question quality:

> Weak, closed and leading: "Do you think that's connected to your dad?"
> Better, open: "What do you make of it?"

> Weak, stacked: "How was the week? Did you do the sleep diary? Was the medication okay?"
> Better: "How was the week?" then wait, then reflect, then ask the next.

> Weak, "why": "Why didn't you go?"
> Better: "What got in the way?" or "Walk me through the hour before you were due to leave."

"Why" questions invite justification and often produce defensiveness or a rationalisation the person then has to defend. "What" and "how" produce description.

Questions that function as advice in disguise are a specific hazard. They allow the clinician to give the advice while appearing to enquire, and clients hear them accurately.

> Disguised advice: "Have you thought about telling her how you feel?"
> Honest version if the advice is genuinely warranted: "I've got a suggestion, and I'm not sure it's right. Do you want it now or do you want to keep going?"

The scaling question is a high-yield open question because the number is less useful than the two follow-ups.

> "Nought to ten, how important is changing this right now?" ... "You said six. Why six and not three?" ... "What would need to happen to move it to seven?"

Asking why the number is not lower pulls change talk. Asking why it is not higher pulls sustain talk, and is the wrong direction unless deliberately used to explore obstacles.

## Silence

Silence is a skill with distinct uses, and the inability to tolerate it is the most reliable marker of an untrained helper. In text-based or conversational AI contexts the equivalent skill is restraint in length: not filling the turn, not adding a second paragraph, not appending a question to a reflection that was complete.

After a question, silence gives the person time to find the real answer rather than the first available one. Most people need three to five seconds of genuine thinking time, and most helpers wait less than two before rephrasing, adding a prompt or supplying an option. The rephrasing is what destroys the answer, because it replaces the person's search with the helper's suggestion.

After the person says something significant, silence lets the significance register rather than being absorbed into the next exchange. A disclosure followed immediately by a clinician's question communicates that the disclosure was a step toward something else.

After crying starts, silence plus visible presence is usually correct. Handing tissues is an ambiguous act, since it can read as care or as a request to stop, and it is worth having them within the person's reach rather than offering them.

Before ending a difficult session, a deliberate pause changes the pace and gives the person a moment to gather themselves before standing up.

While the person is thinking mid-sentence, silence is mandatory. Completing someone's sentence takes the thought away from them.

Silence has failure modes. Extended silence with a person who is anxious, paranoid, or has a history of neglect can be experienced as withdrawal, judgement or punishment, and with a person who has been institutionalised it can read as a test. Silence in a first session, with someone who does not yet know the rules of the encounter, is usually unhelpful and should be short. Silence held because the clinician does not know what to say, extended until it becomes the client's problem to solve, is not a technique.

Naming a silence is often better than either breaking it or extending it, because it makes the pause collaborative rather than something happening to the person.

> "You've gone quiet, and I don't want to guess wrong about what that is."
> "I'm going to leave a gap here rather than fill it, because I think something's still landing."
> "Take as long as you need. I'm not waiting for anything."
> "I notice I want to fill this silence, and I think that's my discomfort rather than yours."

## Validation: the six levels

Linehan's six levels of validation give a graded set of moves, each harder than the last, and each communicating that the person's response makes sense (Linehan, 1997; Linehan, 2015). Validation communicates that a response is understandable, not that it is correct or wise. Validating a self-destructive behaviour's function is not endorsing the behaviour.

V1, paying attention. Being present, oriented, unhurried, and visibly listening. This is validation because attention communicates worth.

> "I've got nowhere else to be for the next forty minutes. Take your time."

V2, accurate reflection. Reflecting back what was said without adding or judging, so the person hears their own account held accurately.

> "So the plan was to go, you got as far as the car park, and you drove home."

V3, articulating the unstated. Naming what the person has not said but is implied by what they have said. This is the level where the clinician guesses, and should hold it lightly.

> "You haven't said this, so tell me if I'm wrong. I imagine there was a moment in the car park where you thought this is going to happen every time."

V4, validating in terms of history or biology. Communicating that the reaction makes sense given the person's learning history or their physiology.

> "Given that the last three times you asked for help you got told you were being dramatic, of course you didn't ring anyone."

V5, validating in terms of present circumstances. Communicating that the reaction is one any reasonable person might have in this situation, independent of the person's history. This is a stronger validation than V4, because V4 says "it makes sense for you given your past" and V5 says "it makes sense full stop."

> "You found out by text, from someone who isn't even family. Anyone would be furious about that."

V6, radical genuineness. Responding to the person as an equal rather than as a patient, including being honest, being affected, and dropping the professional register. Linehan holds this to be the most powerful level, particularly for people whose experience of services has been of being handled.

> "I'm not going to do the therapist thing with this one. What happened to you was wrong, and I'm angry on your behalf."

Choosing the level. Use the highest level that is true. Do not use V4 where V5 is available, because explaining a reaction by the person's history when the situation itself justifies it subtly implies the reaction is a symptom. Do not use V5 or V6 for a response that is genuinely not warranted by the situation, because false validation is detected and costs credibility.

Validation of the invalid is the specific error to avoid. If a person says everyone at work hates them and the evidence does not support it, the clinician validates the feeling and the reasons for it, not the conclusion.

> "I'm not going to agree that they all hate you, because I don't think that's true and you'd know I was managing you. What I do believe is that you felt frozen out on Tuesday, and that when that happens it goes straight to a very old place."

## Invalidation by accident

Most invalidation in clinical conversation is unintentional. It comes from three sources: the urge to fix, the urge to reassure, and the urge to reframe too early. All three are motivated by care, which is why they are hard to notice from the inside and easy to notice from the outside.

The structural feature they share is that each one contradicts, corrects or redirects the person's stated experience. The person has said how it is, and the reply says it is not quite like that. The effect is that the person stops elaborating, either by moving to a safer topic or by agreeing in order to end the exchange. Compliant agreement following a well-meant reassurance is the standard signature of accidental invalidation, and it is easy to mistake for the reassurance having worked.

The corrective in every case is to stay with the content one beat longer before doing anything with it.

> "At least it wasn't worse." Comparison invalidates by implying the person should be measuring against something else.
> Alternative: "That was bad enough on its own terms."

> "Everything happens for a reason." Meaning imposed from outside.
> Alternative: "I don't have a frame that makes this make sense. I don't think there is one."

> "You shouldn't feel guilty, you did everything you could." Instruction about what to feel.
> Alternative: "The guilt's there whether it's fair or not. What is it saying to you?"

> "Try not to think about it." A request to suppress, which reliably increases intrusion.
> Alternative: "What happens when you try not to think about it?"

> "But you've got so much going for you." Contradiction of the person's self-report, offered as encouragement.
> Alternative: "None of that touches it, does it."

> "I completely understand, the same thing happened to me." Redirection to the clinician plus an assumed equivalence.
> Alternative: "I don't know what that was like for you. Tell me."

> "Have you tried mindfulness?" Solution offered to a person who has not asked for one and has probably tried it.
> Alternative: "What have you already tried? I'd rather not suggest something you've been through."

## Immediacy

Immediacy is talking about what is happening between the two people, in the present, out loud. It is used to address something occurring in the relationship that is either an obstacle or a resource: withdrawal, tension, an unexpected warmth, a repeated pattern being enacted.

The structure that works: describe the observable, own it as your perception, invite correction, and stop.

> "Something shifted about ten minutes ago and I've felt further away from you since. I might be wrong about that. Does it match anything for you?"

> "I notice I've been working quite hard in the last few minutes, and you've been giving me short answers. I don't want to just push on. What's going on for you?"

> "I want to say something awkward. When you thanked me at the end last week it felt like you were reassuring me that I was doing okay. I wondered if you look after me in here."

Rules. Use the present tense. Describe behaviour, not character. Do not use immediacy to discharge irritation, which is the most common misuse. Do not use it as an accusation dressed as observation ("I notice you're being defensive"). Check whether the relationship can hold it, since immediacy in session one with a mistrustful person is usually premature.

A qualitative meta-analysis of 21 studies of therapist self-disclosure and immediacy found that immediacy was associated more often with clients opening up and being immediate in return, and that both processes were linked to an enhanced therapy relationship (Hill, Knox and Pinto-Coelho, 2018, Psychotherapy).

## Self-disclosure and its rules

Therapist self-disclosure comes in two forms. Disclosure of facts about the therapist's life outside the room, and disclosure of the therapist's in-the-moment reaction, which overlaps with immediacy. The evidence base is qualitative rather than dose-response, and the studied processes following disclosure include enhanced relationship, improved client functioning, gains in insight, and general helpfulness (Hill, Knox and Pinto-Coelho, 2018).

Working rules.

Disclose for the client's benefit, not the clinician's. If the impulse arises from wanting to be liked, wanting to seem human, or wanting relief from discomfort, do not.

Keep it brief and return the focus. A disclosure that requires the client to attend to the clinician's story has failed.

Do not disclose unresolved material. Disclosing a current struggle transfers the burden.

Do not disclose to demonstrate equivalence. "I know exactly how you feel" is almost never accurate and forecloses the client's account.

Prefer disclosure of process to disclosure of content.

> Poor: "I went through a divorce myself and what helped me was..."
> Better: "I notice I want to reassure you, and I think that's because what you're describing is genuinely hard to sit with. So instead of reassuring you I'm going to stay with it."

> Reasonable content disclosure, brief and returned: "I do have children, yes. I'm aware you asked that just after describing the school run, and I'm curious what it would have meant either way."

Answer direct personal questions with a decision rather than a deflection. Reflexively bouncing every question back ("what would it mean if I did?") is experienced as evasive and, with people who have been managed by institutions, as confirming that this is another such institution.

## Confrontation and challenge done well

Confrontation in a clinical sense means pointing out a discrepancy: between two statements, between stated values and behaviour, between what the person says and what they show. It is not aggression, and it is not telling someone they are wrong.

The workable structure: establish credit first, name the discrepancy descriptively, do not resolve it, hand it back.

> "You've been coming for two months and you've never missed. So I'm going to say something direct, on the basis that you can take it. You've told me every week that the priority is your health, and every week the gym has come after everything else. I don't have an explanation for that. Do you?"

> "Two things you've said today don't sit together. You said you're fine with the arrangement, and you said you've been awake at three every night since it started. I'd like to understand how both are true."

Rules. Confront the discrepancy, not the person. Use their own words as the two poles. Do not add an interpretation of the motive. Do not confront while irritated, since the client will hear the irritation and not the content. Do not confront where the alliance is weak or the person is in crisis. Confront once and then stop, because repeated confrontation is nagging, and in motivational interviewing terms it directly generates sustain talk.

Softening moves that preserve the challenge: prefacing with permission ("can I put something to you?"), attributing the observation to yourself ("I might be reading this wrong"), and offering an exit ("you can tell me that's not it").

What confrontation is not: labelling ("that's denial"), diagnosing in the moment ("that's your avoidant side"), or moralising ("you're going to lose her if you keep this up"). Each of these predicts disengagement.

## Motivational interviewing: spirit, OARS, and the righting reflex

Motivational interviewing (Miller and Rollnick, 2013; 4th edition 2023) is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change. It is defined by its spirit as much as its techniques: partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. Evocation is the load-bearing one, since it means motivation is drawn out rather than installed.

OARS names the four core skills: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries.

Affirmations recognise specific strengths and efforts, and differ from praise in being about the person's actions rather than the clinician's approval.

> Praise: "Well done, I'm proud of you."
> Affirmation: "You rang three services and got fobbed off twice, and you rang the third one anyway. That's persistence."

The righting reflex is the central error the method names: the trained helper's impulse to correct what is wrong, to argue for change, to supply the reasons. The problem is structural. In an ambivalent person, whoever voices the arguments for change causes the other party to voice the arguments against it. A clinician who argues for change reliably produces a client who argues against it, and the client then hears themselves arguing against change, which reduces the likelihood of change.

> Righting reflex: "Look, the reality is if you keep smoking with your lung function where it is, you're going to end up on oxygen. You know that, right?"
> Client: "My grandad smoked until he was ninety."
> MI alternative: "You know more about your lungs than most people do. What's your own read on where this is heading?"

Evidence: a meta-analysis of 119 studies found MI produced statistically significant, durable results in the small effect range against weak comparison conditions (average g = 0.28) and non-significant results against specific active treatments (average g = 0.09) (Lundahl, Kunz, Brownell, Tollefson and Burke, 2010, Research on Social Work Practice). The honest reading is that MI is a good way to start, to engage, and to work with ambivalence, and is not a stand-alone substitute for a specific treatment where one is indicated.

Asking permission before giving information is the standard MI move that keeps the righting reflex in check while still allowing expertise to be shared, often taught as elicit-provide-elicit.

> "Would it be useful if I told you what we know about how this usually goes? ... [information] ... What do you make of that, given your situation?"

## Change talk and sustain talk

MI treats the person's own speech as the mechanism. Change talk is any client language favouring change; sustain talk is client language favouring the status quo. Both are normal parts of ambivalence, and the presence of sustain talk is not resistance.

Preparatory change talk is remembered as DARN: Desire ("I want to"), Ability ("I could"), Reasons ("it would help my sleep"), Need ("I have to"). Mobilising change talk is remembered as CAT: Commitment ("I will"), Activation ("I'm ready to"), Taking steps ("I threw them out on Sunday"). Mobilising language is the better predictor of actual change, and the shift from DARN to CAT is what the clinician is listening for.

The core behavioural rule is that people hear themselves talk, and reflecting a category produces more of that category. Reflect sustain talk and you get more sustain talk. Reflect change talk and you get more change talk. This does not mean ignoring sustain talk, which would be poor listening, but it does mean choosing what to reflect and what to place last in a summary.

> Client: "I know it's not doing me any good, but honestly it's the only thing that gets me through the evenings."
> Reflecting sustain talk: "The evenings would be unbearable without it."
> Reflecting change talk: "Some part of you is already clear it's costing you something."
> Double-sided, change talk last: "It's what gets you through the evenings, and you're the one who said it's not doing you any good."

Evoking change talk deliberately:

> "What worries you about how things are now?"
> "If you did decide to change this, how would you go about it?"
> "What are the best things that could happen if you made this change?"
> "Suppose you don't change anything. What do you think things look like in five years?"
> "What's the most important reason, for you, not for anyone else?"

Responding to change talk when it appears, using the acronym EARS: Elaborate ("tell me more about that"), Affirm ("that took something to say"), Reflect, Summarise.

Sustain talk should be reflected accurately and not argued with. Arguing produces more of it.

## Rolling with discord, and repairing ruptures in the alliance

The fourth edition of MI retains the distinction that replaced the older term resistance: sustain talk is language about the behaviour, while discord is a problem in the relationship, showing up as arguing, interrupting, dismissing, or disengaging. Discord is treated as a signal about the clinician's current approach rather than a client trait.

Responses to discord: reflect it, shift focus, emphasise autonomy, apologise, or come alongside.

> Emphasising autonomy: "It's completely your call. Nobody can make you do this, and I'm not going to try."
> Coming alongside: "Maybe this isn't the right time for any of it. It might be that the sensible thing is to leave it where it is for now."
> Apologising: "I've been pushing. That's on me, not you."

Ruptures in the alliance are a broader phenomenon: a deterioration in the therapeutic alliance, showing as disagreement on goals, lack of collaboration on tasks, or a strain in the bond. Safran and Muran distinguish withdrawal ruptures, where the client moves away by going compliant, vague, intellectual or silent, and confrontation ruptures, where the client moves against by expressing anger, complaint or dissatisfaction.

Withdrawal is harder to spot and more common. Markers: shorter answers, increased agreement without content, storytelling that goes nowhere, changed topic after a difficult moment, cancellations after intense sessions.

The repair sequence, in order: notice, name it non-defensively, invite the client's account, take real responsibility for the clinician's part without over-apologising, and only then explore what it connects to.

> "Can I stop us? I've had a sense in the last two sessions that we've been going through the motions, and I think that started around the time I pushed the homework. I'd rather hear about that than carry on."

> Client: "Honestly, I thought the whole worksheet thing was pointless."
> Poor: "It can feel that way at first, but the evidence is that it helps."
> Better: "Then I got that wrong, and I'm glad you've said so. What would be worth doing with this time instead?"

Evidence: rupture repair is associated with better outcome. A meta-analysis of 11 studies covering 1,314 patients found a relation between rupture repair episodes and treatment outcome of r = .29, d = .62 (95% CI [.10, .47], p = .003), a moderate association (Eubanks, Muran and Safran, 2018, Psychotherapy 55(4):508-519). Ruptures that are noticed and repaired are not therapy going wrong. Ruptures that go unnoticed, and the client's silent decision not to return, are.

The two common clinician errors in repair are defending the technique and over-apologising. Defending confirms the client's sense that the relationship cannot hold criticism. Over-apologising makes the client responsible for managing the clinician's guilt, which for people who grew up managing an adult's feelings reproduces the original problem exactly.

## A catalogue of bad-to-better rewrites

> Client: "My mum died in March and I still can't get through a day without crying."
> Bad: "Grief takes time. There's no timeline, and five months isn't long at all. Have you considered a bereavement group?"
> Better: "Five months, and it's still every day. Tell me about the crying. When does it come?"

> Client: "I hate my job but I can't leave, I've got a mortgage."
> Bad: "It might be worth updating your CV and seeing what's out there. You never know."
> Better: "So it's trapped rather than undecided. How long have you been in that position?"

> Client: "I think I might be a bad person."
> Bad: "You're clearly not. Look at how much you care about your family."
> Better: "That's a heavy thing to be carrying around. What's the evidence you're using?"

> Client: "I didn't do the diary again this week. Sorry."
> Bad: "That's okay. Let's try again this week. Maybe set a reminder on your phone?"
> Better: "No apology needed. I'm more interested in what happened, because that's usually information. When did you last think about doing it?"

> Client: "I don't really want to talk about my dad."
> Bad: "I understand, but it might be important. We can go at your pace."
> Better: "Then we won't. Is it that you don't want to today, or that you don't want to at all? Both are fine answers, they just tell me different things."

> Client: "Do you actually think I can get better?"
> Bad: "Absolutely. I've seen people in much worse situations turn things around."
> Better: "I think what you've got is treatable, and I've seen it treated. I don't know how it'll go for you specifically, and I'd be lying if I said I did. What made you ask now?"

> Client: "I had a few drinks before I came, actually."
> Bad: "Right. Well, that's not ideal for the work, is it."
> Better: "Thanks for telling me, that can't have been easy to say. How are you feeling in yourself right now?"

> Client: "Everyone would be better off without me."
> Bad: "That's the depression talking. Your family love you."
> Better: "I want to slow right down, because that's an important thing to have said. When you say better off without you, are you thinking about ending your life?"

The last pair matters most. Reassurance in response to a suicidal statement closes the disclosure. A direct, calm, specific question opens it, and asking directly about suicide does not plant the idea.
