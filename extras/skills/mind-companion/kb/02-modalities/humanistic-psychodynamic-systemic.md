## Person-centred therapy: Rogers' six conditions and what they actually require

Rogers (1957) specified six conditions he held to be necessary and sufficient for therapeutic personality change: two persons are in psychological contact; the client is in a state of incongruence, being vulnerable or anxious; the therapist is congruent or integrated in the relationship; the therapist experiences unconditional positive regard for the client; the therapist experiences empathic understanding of the client's internal frame of reference and endeavours to communicate this; and this communication is achieved to at least a minimal degree. Conditions three, four and five are the ones usually named as the core conditions, and condition six is the one most often forgotten, since it makes the client's perception the test rather than the therapist's intention.

The claimed mechanism is the actualising tendency. Rogers held that the organism moves toward growth when the conditions permit it, and that psychological distress arises from conditions of worth, meaning the introjected requirement to be a certain way in order to be valued. Removing conditions of worth in the therapy relationship allows self-experience that was previously denied or distorted to be symbolised accurately, which reduces incongruence between self-concept and experience.

Congruence is the most misread condition. It does not mean saying whatever the therapist feels. It means the therapist's inner experience, awareness of it, and communication are aligned, and that the therapist is not presenting a professional facade. In practice, congruence usually appears as the absence of false warmth rather than as disclosure.

Unconditional positive regard does not mean approval of behaviour. It means the therapist's regard does not fluctuate with what the client reports.

Evidence: therapist empathy is a robust predictor of outcome. A meta-analysis of 82 independent samples and 6,138 clients found a mean weighted r of .28 (95% CI .23 to .33, equivalent to d = .58), with the association holding across theoretical orientations, treatment formats and client problems (Elliott, Bohart, Watson and Murphy, 2018, Psychotherapy). This is evidence that empathy matters in all therapies, not evidence that the conditions are sufficient on their own, and Rogers' sufficiency claim remains contested.

Limits. Person-centred therapy conducted as pure non-directiveness underperforms in presentations where the maintaining mechanism is behavioural, notably OCD, specific phobia and eating disorders, where a purely reflective stance can inadvertently accommodate avoidance. It is also poorly suited as a stand-alone approach where risk is active and containment is required. The most common misapplication in the other direction is reflection used as a technique divorced from actual regard, which clients experience as parroting.

> Client: "I don't even know why I'm here. It all seems pointless."
> Poor: "It sounds like you're feeling hopeless. Have you thought about what might help?"
> Better: "There's something in the way you said that, like even coming here is one more thing that won't make a difference. And you came anyway."

## Emotion-focused therapy: emotion schemes, markers and task guidance

Emotion-focused therapy (Greenberg, Rice and Elliott, 1993; Greenberg, 2011) sits inside the humanistic tradition but is process-directive. The therapist is non-directive about content and highly directive about process, which is the defining move of the model.

The theory distinguishes four kinds of emotional experience. Primary adaptive emotions are direct, useful responses to the situation, such as anger at violation or sadness at loss, and the aim is to access and use them. Primary maladaptive emotions are direct responses that are no longer useful, typically core shame or the fear of abandonment learned early, and the aim is to transform them rather than regulate them away. Secondary emotions are reactions to a primary emotion, such as anger covering hurt, and the aim is to get past them to what they cover. Instrumental emotions are expressed to influence others and the aim is to make their function explicit.

The central change principle is that maladaptive emotion is changed by another emotion rather than by reason. Accessing an adaptive emotion, usually assertive anger or self-compassion or grief, transforms a maladaptive core state such as shame or fear.

EFT is organised by markers. The therapist listens for a specific in-session indicator and offers the task that fits it. Self-critical split, meaning the client reports being at war with themselves, indicates two-chair dialogue. Unfinished business with a significant other indicates empty-chair work. An unclear felt sense, where the client knows something is wrong but cannot name it, indicates focusing. A problematic reaction point, where the client's reaction surprised them, indicates systematic evocative unfolding. A self-interruptive split, where the client blocks their own emotion, indicates two-chair enactment of the blocking.

Evidence: EFT has an outcome literature for depression and for interpersonal injury resolution, with trials generally supporting equivalence to CBT for depression and some advantage on interpersonal and self-esteem outcomes. Precise pooled effect sizes vary across reviews and should be checked against the specific meta-analysis being cited before quoting a number [UNVERIFIED as to any single canonical figure].

Limits and misapplications. Chair work run with a client who cannot regulate afterwards is the main risk, and it is a real one, because the tasks are designed to raise arousal. Doing chairwork in a single session with no continuity is unsafe. EFT is contraindicated as an early intervention in acute psychosis and requires substantial adaptation where dissociation is present. Offering a task without the marker present produces a mechanical enactment with no affective engagement, which is the most common training error.

## EFT chairwork: verbatim wording for two-chair and empty-chair

Two-chair work for a self-critical split. The client sits in one chair as the critic and speaks to the self in the other chair, then swaps and responds from the criticised self. The therapist keeps the two voices separated, keeps the language in the second person, and pulls for specificity and for feeling.

> Clinician: "You said a moment ago that part of you is disgusted with you. I'd like to make that concrete rather than talk about it. Would you be willing to sit here and say that to yourself, as if you were sitting over there?"
> Client (as critic): "You're pathetic. You had one thing to do."
> Clinician: "Say it again, and tell them what you want them to feel."
> Client (as critic): "You're pathetic. You should be ashamed."
> Clinician: "Now come over here. What happens in you when you hear that?"
> Client (as self): "My chest goes tight. I want to disappear."
> Clinician: "Stay with the tight chest. Tell them what it does to you when they speak to you like that. Not what you think about it. What it does."
> Client: "It makes me want to give up entirely."
> Clinician: "Can you tell them that? 'When you say that to me, I want to give up.'"

The therapeutic target is a softening in the critic, which usually appears as the critic expressing fear or need rather than contempt, and an assertive or self-compassionate response from the criticised self. The therapist listens for these and does not push past them.

Empty-chair work for unfinished business. The client addresses an absent or deceased figure.

> Clinician: "Put your father in that chair. Not the man now, the man as he was when you were eleven. What do you see?"
> Client: "He's not looking at me."
> Clinician: "Tell him what it was like that he wasn't looking at you."
> Client: "I don't know if I can."
> Clinician: "That's honest. Can you tell him that? 'I don't know if I can say this to you.'"

The therapist follows arousal, slows the pace when affect rises, and does not require a resolution. Forced forgiveness is a serious error in this work: the aim is that the client accesses and expresses the previously blocked primary emotion, and holds the other accountable or lets go, and either outcome is acceptable.

Ending the task matters as much as starting it. The therapist brings the client back to their own chair, out of the enactment, and spends time on what just happened before the session closes.

> "Come back over here and take a breath. That was a lot. Before we finish, what stands out from what you just did?"

## Existential therapy: the givens, the stance, the evidence

Existential therapy is a stance and a set of concerns more than a technique set. Yalom (1980) organised it around four ultimate concerns: death, freedom and the responsibility that accompanies it, existential isolation, and meaninglessness. Frankl's logotherapy centres meaning specifically, developed from his account of survival in the camps. The claimed mechanism is that avoidance of these givens generates symptoms, and that confronting them directly, with the therapist as fellow traveller rather than expert, permits an authentic reorganisation of how the person lives.

Technique is deliberately thin. The therapist works in the present, attends to how the client is avoiding choice, names responsibility without blame, and uses the relationship as a live instance of connection in the face of isolation. There is little homework and no protocol.

> "You've described this as something that happened to you. I want to put a harder question. What are you choosing, by not deciding?"

> "You said you have no options. I don't think that's true, and I don't think you think it is either. I think you have options you don't want, and that's a different kind of terrible."

Evidence: the general existential literature is thin on randomised trials. The exception is meaning-centred psychotherapy for existential distress in advanced cancer, which has been tested in randomised controlled trials. A trial randomising 253 patients with advanced cancer to eight-session manualised meaning-centred group psychotherapy or supportive group psychotherapy supported the efficacy of the meaning-centred condition for psychological and existential distress, and a subsequent trial of individual meaning-centred psychotherapy found small to moderate effects versus usual care and smaller effects versus supportive psychotherapy (Breitbart et al., 2015; Breitbart et al., 2018, Cancer). Dignity therapy and life review interventions have a parallel small literature in palliative settings.

Limits. Existential work assumes a client with enough cognitive and affective capacity to tolerate uncertainty and abstraction. It is not a first-line approach for acute crisis, for active psychosis, or for a person who needs symptom relief in order to function this week. Its most common misapplication is philosophical conversation that is engaging for both parties and changes nothing, which is difficult to detect because both people enjoy it. The corrective is to check whether anything in the client's actual life has changed.

## Psychodynamic therapy: unconscious process, defence, transference and the frame

Contemporary psychodynamic therapy claims that current difficulties are shaped by patterns formed in early relationships, that much of this operates outside awareness, and that these patterns are enacted in the therapy relationship where they can be observed live and revised. The mechanism is insight combined with corrective relational experience, with the field increasingly emphasising the second over the first.

Shedler's (2010) summary of distinctive features is a useful working list: focus on affect and expression of emotion, exploration of attempts to avoid distressing thoughts and feelings, identification of recurring themes and patterns, discussion of past experience, focus on interpersonal relations, focus on the therapy relationship, and exploration of fantasy life.

Defence is understood functionally: a defence is what a person does to avoid an unbearable feeling. The clinician names the defence before naming the feeling, because interpreting the feeling while the defence is operating produces intellectual agreement without contact. Common defences to recognise in transcript: intellectualisation (talking about feelings in the abstract), rationalisation, displacement, projection, reaction formation, denial, isolation of affect (recounting a distressing event with no emotional tone), somatisation, and acting out.

Transference is the client's experience of the therapist patterned by earlier relationships. Countertransference is the therapist's response, and modern usage treats it as information about the client's relational world rather than only as a therapist problem. The frame is the set of arrangements around the therapy, time, fee, boundaries, and the frame is treated as clinically meaningful, so that consistent lateness or the request to extend a session becomes material rather than administration.

Evidence: a Cochrane review of short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy for common mental disorders included 23 studies with 1,431 randomised patients and found significant improvement versus controls, with fixed-effects standardised mean differences in the short term of -0.42 (95% CI -0.58 to -0.27) for general psychiatric symptoms, -0.47 (95% CI -0.61 to -0.33) for depressive symptoms, -0.46 (95% CI -0.64 to -0.27) for anxiety, and -0.67 (95% CI -0.85 to -0.48) for somatic symptoms, with gains generally maintained at medium and long-term follow-up. The authors concluded that STPP "shows promise, with modest to moderate, often sustained gains", and cautioned that few studies contributed to each category, that heterogeneity was significant, and that results were not always maintained in sensitivity analyses (Abbass, Hancock, Henderson and Kisely, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2006, Issue 4, CD004687).

Leichsenring and Rabung's (2004) meta-analysis reported large pre-post effect sizes for STPP of 1.39 for target problems, 0.90 for general psychiatric symptoms and 0.80 for social functioning, increasing at follow-up. Pre-post effect sizes are not the same as controlled between-group effects and should not be compared with the Cochrane figures above. Their later meta-analysis of long-term psychodynamic psychotherapy in complex disorders has been the subject of substantial methodological criticism, and claims resting on it should be qualified.

NICE lists short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy among the options for less severe depression in adults, and among the psychological treatments to be considered in combination with an antidepressant for depression with a coexisting personality disorder diagnosis (NICE guideline NG222, 2022).

## Malan's triangles and short-term dynamic work

Malan's two triangles are the working map of short-term dynamic therapy, refined in Davanloo's intensive short-term dynamic psychotherapy (ISTDP).

The triangle of conflict has three corners: defence, anxiety, and hidden feeling or impulse. The hidden feeling presses toward awareness, which generates anxiety, which activates a defence. The clinician's job is to move from defence, through anxiety, to feeling. Attempting to reach the feeling without addressing the defence produces resistance.

The triangle of person has three corners: the therapist, current or recent others, and past figures, usually parents. The clinician tracks the same conflict as it appears in each. Malan's formulation is that a full interpretation links a corner of the triangle of conflict across corners of the triangle of person.

In ISTDP specifically the clinician also tracks anxiety pathways in the body, distinguishing striated muscle tension (which indicates the client can tolerate the rising feeling), smooth muscle activation such as gut symptoms or migraine, and cognitive-perceptual disruption such as blurred vision or dissociation. The last two indicate the clinician should regulate the pace down rather than press further, and treating all anxiety as workable is the characteristic error in this method.

> Defence identification: "I notice that when I ask what you felt, you tell me what you thought. That happens every time we get close. Do you see it too?"

> Defence clarification with cost: "That vagueness is protecting you from something. It's also the reason you can't get anywhere with this on your own. What do you want to do with it?"

> Anxiety regulation: "Let's slow down. Where are you feeling this in your body right now?"

> Linking across the triangle of person: "You said you couldn't bring it up with your manager because she'd think you were difficult. That's what you said about your mother two weeks ago. And I'd add a third: you waited four sessions to tell me the times don't work for you."

## Interpretation: how it is worded and when to withhold it

An interpretation proposes a meaning the client has not stated. It is the highest-risk verbal act in dynamic work, because a premature or wrong interpretation can be experienced as an accusation or as the therapist knowing the client better than the client knows themselves.

Rules of wording. Offer it tentatively and leave room for rejection. Ground it in material the client provided in this session or a recent one. Make it short. Stop after it and wait rather than elaborating. Treat disagreement as data about the interpretation as well as about the client, not automatically as resistance.

> Weak: "You're angry with me because I represent your father."
> Better: "I want to try something out and you should tell me if it doesn't fit. When I said we'd need to end on time, something changed in the room. I wondered if it felt like being managed rather than met, which is close to what you've described about home."

The distinction that matters most in practice is between an interpretation and an observation. Observations are lower-risk and usually more useful early: they describe something visible without asserting a cause.

> Observation: "You smiled when you said that, and what you said wasn't funny."
> Interpretation: "I think the smile is there to make it easier for me to hear, so I won't be burdened by it."

When not to interpret. When the alliance is not established. When the client is in acute crisis and needs containment. When the interpretation would primarily demonstrate the therapist's cleverness. When the client is actively dissociating, since insight cannot be integrated in that state. When the material is a current external threat, where interpreting the client's response can obscure the reality of the danger. In brief work with a concrete goal, interpretation should be sparse and tied to the focus.

Transference interpretation specifically should be used sparingly and is dose-sensitive. Clients with fragile ego strength or low reflective capacity generally do worse with frequent transference interpretation, and supportive-expressive work is better indicated for them.

## Attachment-based work

Attachment theory (Bowlby, 1969; Ainsworth) proposes internal working models of self and other formed in early caregiving that shape later expectation and regulation. The clinical translation across models is that the therapist functions as a temporary secure base, and that reliability, repair and affect regulation in the relationship are the active ingredients rather than content.

Common clinical categories, used descriptively rather than diagnostically: secure, anxious-preoccupied, avoidant-dismissing, and disorganised. The practical value is in predicting how the person will use the therapy. A preoccupied presentation tends to produce high affect, difficulty ending sessions, and distress at breaks. A dismissing presentation tends to produce minimised affect, brief answers, and reports that everything is fine, and the risk is that the therapist mistakes this for progress. A disorganised presentation tends to produce approach and withdrawal in the same session, and the therapist's task is predictability rather than depth.

Attachment-based family therapy (ABFT) is the manualised model with the strongest trial support in this group. It has five tasks: relational reframe, adolescent alliance, parent alliance, attachment repair, and promoting autonomy. In a randomised trial of 66 suicidal adolescents comparing ABFT with enhanced usual care over three months, ABFT produced significantly greater change in self-reported suicidal ideation with an effect size of 0.97, with 87% versus 51.7% meeting clinical recovery criteria at post-treatment and 70% versus 34.6% at follow-up (Diamond et al., 2010). A later trial of 129 suicidal adolescents comparing ABFT with family-enhanced non-directive supportive therapy found no significant between-group difference in rate of change in suicidal ideation (Diamond et al., 2019), which is the honest qualifier on the earlier result: ABFT beats usual care clearly, and beats a well-delivered supportive alternative less clearly.

Relational reframe wording, which shifts the target from the adolescent's symptom to the relationship:

> "I want to suggest something different from what you came in expecting. Rather than working on getting Maya to stop, I want to work on why Maya doesn't come to you when she's in that much pain. Because if she could, a lot of this would look different. Would you be willing to look at that with me?"

Limits. Attachment language is often used loosely as personality description. Adult attachment classifications from research instruments do not transfer cleanly to clinical labelling, and telling a client they are "an avoidant" is not a clinical intervention. Attachment-informed work also cannot substitute for trauma processing or for behavioural treatment where those are indicated.

## Internal Family Systems and parts work

IFS (Schwartz, 1995) holds that the mind is naturally multiple, that all parts have positive intent, and that beneath the parts is a Self characterised by qualities including calm, curiosity, compassion and clarity. Parts are grouped into exiles, which carry pain and are kept out of awareness; managers, which run the person's life pre-emptively to keep exiles down; and firefighters, which act reactively and often destructively when an exile breaks through, covering behaviours such as bingeing, substance use, self-harm and dissociation. The claimed mechanism is unburdening: with Self-leadership established, protective parts give permission to access exiles, the exile's burden is witnessed and released, and protectors take up new roles.

The characteristic sequence is often taught as six steps: find the part, focus on it, flesh it out, feel toward it, befriend it, and explore its fears. Access to Self is checked by asking how the client feels toward the part, and any answer other than curiosity or compassion indicates another part is present and blended.

> Clinician: "Where do you notice the part that wants to shut this down?"
> Client: "It's like a wall across my chest."
> Clinician: "How do you feel toward the wall?"
> Client: "Frustrated. I want it gone."
> Clinician: "That frustration is another part. Can we ask it to give us a little space, just so we can get to know the wall? It doesn't have to leave. It can watch."

> Asking a protector its fear: "If it didn't do that job any more, what does it think would happen?"

Evidence: IFS was listed on the SAMHSA National Registry of Evidence-Based Programs and Practices in 2015, rated effective for improving general functioning and well-being and promising for depression and anxiety. The registry has since been discontinued, which limits how much weight the listing carries. A pilot effectiveness study of 17 adults with PTSD and multiple childhood traumas, receiving sixteen 90-minute IFS sessions, reported reductions in PTSD symptoms, but it was an uncontrolled feasibility study rather than a randomised trial (Hodgdon et al., 2021). The accurate statement is that IFS is promising and under-tested, not that it is an established treatment for PTSD.

Limits. Parts language can reify dissociation in clients with dissociative identity disorder, where the standard phase-based approach and specialist consultation are indicated rather than enthusiastic parts work. Approaching exiles before protectors have consented is the model's own named error and produces backlash, typically an escalation in firefighter behaviour after the session.

## Narrative therapy

Narrative therapy (White and Epston, 1990) holds that people live by stories, that problem-saturated dominant stories obscure exceptions, and that identity is socially and politically constructed rather than internal. The claimed mechanism is that separating the person from the problem and thickening an alternative story changes what the person believes is possible and available.

Techniques. Externalising conversations relocate the problem outside the person and give it a name, often the client's own. Mapping the effects of the problem across life domains. Identifying unique outcomes, meaning occasions when the problem did not win. Re-authoring conversations that build the alternative story across time. Re-membering conversations that recruit significant figures into the preferred identity. Definitional ceremony and outsider witness practices, in which others reflect on what they heard. Therapeutic documents and letters.

> Externalising: "How long has the Worry been running the mornings? And when it does that, what does it tell you about yourself?"

> Mapping effects: "What has the Worry cost you at work? What has it cost between you and Ines?"

> Unique outcome: "On Tuesday you went anyway. That's the first time in a month the Worry didn't get the last word. How did you do that?"

> Re-authoring: "What does that tell us about what you're committed to, that the Worry hasn't been able to take?"

Evidence: the outcome literature is small and methodologically weak. A systematic review screening 461 studies from 1995 to 2021 found 17 meeting inclusion criteria, and reviewers characterise the supporting studies as mostly small, uncontrolled and single-site. Later pooled analyses report large effects but rate the certainty of the evidence as very low, because of risk of bias and lack of blinding. Narrative therapy should be described as promising and under-evidenced rather than established.

Limits. Externalising can minimise responsibility where accountability is the clinical issue, notably in work with people who have used violence, and narrative practitioners working in that field explicitly restructure the method to avoid it. Externalising is also poorly suited to presentations where the mechanism is physiological or behavioural and where naming the problem does not change the contingencies.

## Solution-focused brief therapy

SFBT (de Shazer, Berg) is built on a deliberate refusal to analyse the problem. The premise is that problem talk and solution talk are different conversations, that change is constant, and that solutions do not have to be logically related to problems. The claimed mechanism is that attention to exceptions and to a described preferred future amplifies existing competence and produces small changes that cascade.

Core techniques with wording:

> Miracle question: "Suppose tonight, while you're asleep, a miracle happens and the problem that brought you here is solved. You don't know it happened because you were asleep. What's the first small thing you'd notice tomorrow morning that would tell you something was different?"

> Exception-finding: "When was the last time this was even slightly less of a problem? What was different about that day?"

> Scaling: "On a scale of nought to ten, where ten is the day after the miracle and nought is the worst it's been, where are you today?" followed by "What's keeping you at a four rather than a two?" and "What would a five look like? Not a ten. A five."

> Coping question, used when the client is at the bottom of the scale: "Given everything you've described, how have you managed to keep going at all this week?"

> Compliment and task: "I'm struck that you got yourself here on a day like this. Between now and next time, I'd like you to notice anything that happens that you'd want to continue."

Evidence: a systematic review of 43 randomised and quasi-experimental studies found 32 (74%) reporting significant positive benefit and 10 (23%) reporting positive trends, with the strongest evidence in adult depression where four studies found SFBT comparable to established alternative treatments, and with three studies finding SFBT used fewer sessions than comparison therapies (Gingerich and Peterson, 2013, Research on Social Work Practice).

Limits and misapplications. SFBT's refusal to explore the problem becomes a liability where risk, trauma or abuse is present and needs to be assessed rather than reframed. Scaling a client who has just disclosed sexual assault is a serious failure of fit. SFBT is also poorly suited where a specific evidence-based protocol is indicated, such as ERP for OCD. The most common misapplication is the miracle question deployed on a client in acute despair, which reads as dismissive; the coping question exists precisely for that situation and should be used instead.

## Systemic and family therapy

Systemic therapy treats the problem as located in patterns of interaction rather than in an individual, and treats the presenting person as the identified patient rather than the site of pathology. Circular causality replaces linear causality: A's behaviour maintains B's behaviour which maintains A's.

The traditions differ. Structural family therapy (Minuchin) works on boundaries, hierarchy and subsystems, and uses enactment, meaning the therapist asks the family to have the interaction in the room rather than describe it. Strategic therapy (Haley) uses directives and paradox. Milan systemic work introduced hypothesising, circularity and neutrality, and the technique of circular questioning. Post-Milan and social constructionist approaches added reflecting teams and greater transparency.

Circular questioning is the signature move: asking one family member about the relationship between two others, which surfaces the pattern rather than the position.

> "When your dad and your sister argue about curfew, what does your mum do?"
> "Who in the family worries most about Tom? Who worries second most?"
> "If your brother were sitting here, what would he say the argument is really about?"
> "When things got worse last spring, who noticed first? Who noticed last?"

Enactment:

> "Rather than telling me about it, could you have that conversation now, the two of you, and I'll watch? Start with what you said in the car."

Evidence: family therapy has the strongest guideline standing in specific presentations rather than as a general modality. Anorexia nervosa in children and young people: NICE recommends anorexia-nervosa-focused family therapy (FT-AN), typically 18 to 20 sessions over one year, emphasising the family's role in recovery, avoiding blame, including psychoeducation about nutrition and malnutrition, and supporting parents or carers to take a central role in managing eating (NICE guideline NG69, 2017). For adults with anorexia nervosa the same guideline recommends individual eating-disorder-focused CBT, MANTRA or specialist supportive clinical management, and recommends against medication as sole treatment. Psychosis and schizophrenia: NICE recommends family intervention for people in contact with their families, alongside CBT for psychosis delivered over at least sixteen planned sessions (NICE guideline CG178, 2014). Behavioural couples therapy appears in the NICE depression guideline for people whose relationship is contributing to maintenance (NICE guideline NG222, 2022).

Limits. Systemic work is contraindicated in its conjoint form where there is intimate partner violence, because joint sessions can increase risk to the victim between appointments and because neutrality is not an appropriate stance toward abuse. Neutrality more broadly is a poor fit wherever there is a power differential involving harm, including child protection concerns. Family work with an adult client whose family is the source of ongoing harm requires the clinician to be clear whose treatment this is. Convening a family session because the individual work has stalled, without a systemic formulation, generally produces one difficult session and no change.
