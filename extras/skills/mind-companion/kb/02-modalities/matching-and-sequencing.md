## The decision inputs: what actually determines the choice

Modality selection is not a lookup from diagnosis to therapy. Five inputs combine.

The presentation and its maintaining mechanism. The formulation question is what keeps this going now, not what caused it. Avoidance maintaining anxiety indicates exposure. Withdrawal and loss of reinforcement maintaining depression indicates behavioural activation. Perseverative thinking maintaining distress indicates metacognitive or rumination-focused work. Interpersonal patterns maintaining isolation indicate interpersonal or dynamic work. Family interaction maintaining a young person's restriction indicates family therapy. Two clients with the same diagnosis can have different maintaining mechanisms and should get different treatments.

Guideline standing. Where a guideline recommends a specific treatment for a specific presentation, departing from it requires a documented reason. This is both a clinical and a governance matter.

Risk and stability. Acute risk, unstable housing, active dependence, or an ongoing threat move the question from which therapy to what has to be in place first.

Client preference and prior experience. Preference predicts engagement, and engagement predicts outcome. A client who has already failed a competently delivered course of CBT and disliked it should not be offered the same thing again by default.

Capacity and context. Session number available, the client's cognitive and literacy profile, language, whether they have privacy for online sessions, whether they can do between-session tasks, whether interpreters are needed, whether the therapy is time-limited by the service.

A sixth input is what the clinician can actually deliver competently. Referring on is a clinical decision, not an admission of failure, and delivering a poor imitation of a specialist treatment is worse than referring.

> "There are two or three reasonable ways to work with this, and they feel quite different in the room. I'd like to describe them and hear which one you'd actually turn up for, because that matters more than which one the research prefers by a nose."

## Presentation-to-modality matching: what the guidelines actually say

Depression in adults. NICE recommends a menu of options ordered by the committee, differing for less severe and more severe depression, with individual CBT and behavioural activation among the options in both, alongside guided self-help, group interventions, interpersonal psychotherapy, short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy, counselling and antidepressant medication depending on severity and preference. For depression with a coexisting personality disorder diagnosis, a combination of an antidepressant and a psychological treatment such as behavioural activation, CBT, interpersonal psychotherapy or short-term psychodynamic psychotherapy should be considered. Behavioural couples therapy appears as an option where the relationship contributes to maintenance (NICE guideline NG222, 2022).

PTSD. Trauma-focused CBT should be offered to adults with a diagnosis or clinically important symptoms of PTSD presenting more than one month after the traumatic event. EMDR should be offered to adults presenting more than three months after a non-combat-related trauma. For children and young people aged 7 to 17, EMDR should be considered only where they do not respond to or engage with trauma-focused CBT (NICE guideline NG116, 2018). The 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline recommends prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy or EMDR, recommends manualised psychotherapy over pharmacotherapy, and recommends against benzodiazepines and cannabis products.

OCD. Treatment intensity is matched to functional impairment. Low-intensity psychological treatment including exposure and response prevention, up to 10 therapist hours, should be offered where impairment is mild or the person prefers a low-intensity approach, with more intensive CBT including ERP for greater impairment. For obsessive thoughts without overt compulsions, CBT including exposure to the thoughts and prevention of mental rituals and neutralising should be considered. Cognitive therapy adapted for OCD may be added to ERP. A family member or carer may be involved as co-therapist where appropriate (NICE guideline CG31, 2005).

Psychosis and schizophrenia. CBT for psychosis should be delivered over at least 16 planned sessions, one to one. Family intervention should be offered to people in contact with their families (NICE guideline CG178, 2014).

Eating disorders. For children and young people with anorexia nervosa, anorexia-nervosa-focused family therapy is recommended, typically 18 to 20 sessions over one year. For adults with anorexia nervosa, individual eating-disorder-focused CBT, MANTRA or specialist supportive clinical management. Medication should not be offered as the sole treatment (NICE guideline NG69, 2017).

Borderline personality disorder. A comprehensive dialectical behaviour therapy programme should be considered for women for whom reducing recurrent self-harm is a priority. Brief psychological interventions of less than three months' duration should not be used specifically for borderline personality disorder or its individual symptoms (NICE guideline CG78, 2009).

The APA Division 12 database of research-supported psychological treatments provides a parallel US-based resource, rated under criteria from Tolin et al. (2015) as very strong, strong, weak or insufficient, with an archived older list rated under Chambless and Hollon (1998). It should be consulted directly rather than quoted from memory, since ratings have changed over time and some were revised downward.

## Stepped care and matched care

Stepped care allocates the least intensive intervention likely to produce benefit first, with systematic monitoring and a route to step up. In the English implementation the steps are: step 1, recognition, assessment and active monitoring; step 2, low-intensity interventions such as guided self-help and computerised CBT delivered by psychological wellbeing practitioners; step 3, high-intensity psychological therapy delivered by qualified therapists, and medication; step 4, specialist and crisis services, inpatient care, and combined treatment for severe and complex presentations including risk to life and severe self-neglect.

Two failure modes of stepped care matter clinically.

Under-treatment by default. Stepping a person through low-intensity work when the presentation clearly indicates high-intensity treatment wastes months and teaches the person that treatment does not work. Guidelines allow direct allocation to the appropriate step, and matched care means starting where the presentation indicates rather than at the bottom.

Failure to step up. Stepped care depends on monitoring with a route out. Without session-by-session measurement and a review point, people sit at step 2 indefinitely.

The practical marker for stepping up is absence of change on a routine measure after an adequate dose. For low-intensity work an adequate dose is generally in the region of six sessions before a review, though this is a service convention rather than a trial-derived threshold [UNVERIFIED as a specific number].

Presentations that should generally not start at low intensity: PTSD, OCD with significant impairment, anorexia nervosa, psychosis, bipolar disorder, and any presentation with active suicidal intent.

## The first session: what it must accomplish

A first session has to do six things, and a clinician who runs out of time will usually have spent it on history at the expense of the rest.

Establish enough safety for the person to speak. This includes the practical frame: length, frequency, confidentiality and its limits, note-keeping, what happens if they cannot attend.

Hear the problem in the person's own words before structuring it. The opening should be open and the clinician should not intervene early.

> "Where would you like to start?"
> "What made you pick up the phone when you did, rather than three months ago?"

Assess risk. Directly, calmly, specifically, and not at the very end when there is no time to respond.

Get a provisional formulation, meaning a working account of what keeps this going, held loosely.

Agree a focus. Not a full treatment plan, but a shared statement of what the work is about. A person who leaves the first session without knowing what they are working on is more likely not to return.

Instil some hope, honestly. Not by promising outcomes but by making the problem legible.

> "What you've described has a shape to it, and it's a shape I recognise. The avoidance is doing most of the work of keeping it going, and that's the part that responds well to treatment."

What not to do in a first session: deep exploration of trauma detail, chairwork or other high-arousal experiential tasks, transference interpretation, or a homework assignment the person has not shaped. Asking for the trauma narrative in session one, when there is no containment and no relationship, is the classic harmful first-session error.

Closing the first session:

> "We're coming to time. Before we stop, I want to check what this has been like, and I'd particularly like to know anything that felt off or that I got wrong."

## The fifth session: what should be different

By roughly session five the work should have changed in character. If session five looks like session one, that is a signal.

What should be present. A shared formulation the client can state in their own words. An agreed focus that has survived a few weeks. Some between-session activity, however small. Measurable movement on something, even if the primary symptom has not shifted. A relationship robust enough to survive disagreement.

What the session itself looks like. Less history-gathering, more work on a specific instance. The bridge from last session and the review of between-session work take up real time rather than being a formality. The clinician does less structuring because the client brings material that fits the focus.

> Session one: "Tell me about the panic attacks."
> Session five: "You had one on Thursday. Let's take that one apart minute by minute, because you also had a near-miss on Saturday that didn't turn into one, and the difference is where the information is."

By session five, the clinician should also be able to answer three questions. What is the mechanism I am targeting. What would count as evidence that this is working. What would change my mind about the formulation.

This is also the standard point for an explicit review.

> "We're five in. I want to step back for ten minutes rather than carry on by momentum. What's better, what's the same, and what do you want us to do differently for the next stretch?"

If the answer is that nothing is different, that is a finding, and it belongs in the room rather than in the clinician's private worry.

## The phase-based trauma model

Herman's (1992) three-stage model remains the organising frame for complex trauma work: safety and stabilisation, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection. The ISTSS expert consensus guidelines for complex PTSD in adults set out the same structure: phase one prioritises safety, symptom reduction, and emotional, social and psychological skills; phase two processes unresolved traumatic memories for integration; phase three consolidates gains and supports re-engagement with relationships, work, education and community.

Phase one content. Physical and situational safety first, meaning housing, violence, finances and substance use. Then affect regulation skills, distress tolerance, grounding, sleep, and a workable daily structure. Then psychoeducation about trauma responses, which reframes symptoms as understandable rather than as evidence of being broken. Then relational safety in the therapy itself, which for people harmed in relationships takes longer than clinicians expect.

Phase two content. Structured processing of the traumatic material using a specific method rather than open-ended recounting. Repeated unstructured retelling without a processing framework is retraumatising and should not be mistaken for phase two work.

Phase three content. Grief for what was lost, identity beyond the trauma, relationships, work, and the practical business of a life. This phase is routinely truncated by service limits and is where much of the durable gain sits.

Evidence and controversy. Cloitre et al. (2010, American Journal of Psychiatry 167:915-924) compared phase-based skills training followed by exposure against exposure-focused and skills-focused conditions in PTSD related to childhood abuse and found results favouring the phase-based approach over the exposure-focused condition. Against this, a body of work argues that stabilisation is often unnecessary and that immediate trauma-focused treatment is tolerated and effective in complex populations, including a randomised clinical trial comparing phase-based treatment with immediate trauma-focused treatment for PTSD due to childhood abuse published in BJPsych Open, and a critical analysis of complex PTSD guidelines by De Jongh and colleagues. The direction and magnitude of results in that specific trial should be checked against the paper before being asserted [UNVERIFIED].

The defensible current position is that phase-based sequencing is a reasonable default for complex presentations with poor affect regulation, active self-harm or dissociation, and that indefinite stabilisation which never proceeds to processing is a common and harmful failure, not a cautious one.

## When to stabilise before processing: the decision criteria

The question is not whether the person's history is severe. It is whether they can enter high arousal, do something useful in it, and come down again within the session and afterwards.

Indicators to stabilise first. Current self-harm at a frequency or severity that would be dangerous if it escalated. Active suicidal intent or planning. Substance use that would be the likely response to increased distress. Dissociation that the person cannot interrupt, including amnesia for parts of sessions. No capacity to self-soothe, meaning the person cannot name a single thing that reliably helps. Living with the perpetrator or in ongoing danger. Acute psychotic symptoms. Recent inpatient admission. No support at all between appointments combined with a long gap between them. Severe untreated eating disorder where medical stability is at risk.

Indicators that processing can start. The person can identify and use at least one grounding or regulation strategy in session. They can report distress rising and falling rather than only being at ceiling. They have somewhere to go and someone or something to do after sessions. Substance use is not the primary regulation method. Risk is present but not acute and there is a plan.

Practical tests worth running before committing to processing:

> "I'd like to try something small. I'm going to ask you to bring the memory to mind for about thirty seconds, not the worst part, just the edge of it. Then we'll stop and I want to see how quickly you can come back. This is a test of the brakes, not the engine."

> "Between now and next week, what will you do in the two hours after a session like that? I'd rather we planned it than hoped."

The stabilisation trap. Some clients can stay in phase one indefinitely because it is tolerable and processing is not. The clinician's own anxiety contributes. The correction is a stated time frame and a review point.

> "We've been building the foundations for three months and you've got the skills. I don't want us to stay here because it's comfortable, because that would leave the thing you came for untouched. I'd like to plan the next phase now, with a date."

## When not to go deep

Depth is not a virtue in itself. There are situations where the correct clinical action is to stay on the surface.

When containment is what is needed. Acute crisis, recent bereavement in the first days, immediately after a disclosure to police or child protection. The work is practical and supportive.

When the session structure cannot hold it. A single session, a duty appointment, a phone call, the last ten minutes of any session. Opening material there is a clinician's error, and the standard preventive is to watch the clock and to decline material that cannot be closed.

> "I want to hear this properly, and we've got eight minutes, which isn't enough to open it and put it back down safely. Can we make it the first thing next week?"

When the person is not stable enough. See the criteria above.

When the person has said no. A stated boundary is clinical information and is respected.

When going deep would serve the clinician. Curiosity, the wish to be the one who reaches the material, and the pull of an interesting case are all real and all recognisable in supervision.

When the presenting problem is external. Debt, immigration insecurity, housing, workplace bullying, domestic abuse. Psychological exploration of a person's response to a real ongoing threat, in place of practical action, is a category error and can be actively harmful.

When the person is high-risk and about to leave. Never open new material in the final quarter of a session with someone at risk.

## Pacing and titration

The working concept is arousal within a usable range: high enough that the material is emotionally live, low enough that the person can think. Above the range, the person is flooded, and no new learning is encoded. Below it, the person is numb, intellectualising or dissociated, and again nothing is encoded. The clinician's task is to keep the work in the band and to move the person back into it when they leave it.

Markers of going over. Speech speeding up or stopping. Breathing changes. Visible shaking. Losing the thread mid-sentence. Eyes fixed or unfocused. Reports of the room going far away or of not feeling their hands. Answering questions with long delays.

Markers of going under. Flat recounting of atrocity. Talking about the self in the third person or in general terms. Sudden humour. Detailed factual narration with no affect. Repeated "I don't know" delivered without searching.

Titration moves. Reduce the sensory detail requested. Shift tense from present to past. Move from image to word. Add distance ("watch it on a screen you can pause"). Split the memory and take one part. Shorten the exposure and increase the frequency. Interleave a regulation task. Return to the room by naming objects or by orienting to the present.

> "Stop there. Feet on the floor. Tell me four things in this room that are blue."
> "You've gone quite far in. Come back to me. Say my name and tell me what day it is."
> "We're going to take that one memory and cut it into three. Today we only do the first part, the drive there. We don't get out of the car."

The end of a session is a technical matter, not a courtesy. Processing work needs a deliberate closing that takes real minutes: return to the present, check state, review what will happen in the next few hours, confirm risk status.

> "Before you go. Where are you on a scale of nought to ten right now, and where were you when you came in? What are you doing straight after this? Who's in the house tonight?"

## Homework and between-session work

Therapy happens for one hour a week and life happens in the other 167. Between-session work is how the therapy reaches the life. Meta-analytic work in CBT associates homework completion with better outcome, and the association holds across studies, though the causal direction is not fully settled and the precise pooled effect size should be checked against the specific meta-analysis being cited (Kazantzis and colleagues) [UNVERIFIED as to the exact figure].

Design rules that determine whether it happens.

Collaborative, not assigned. The client should generate or at least shape the task.

Specific to the point of triviality. When, where, how long, with what. "Do some exercise" fails. "Walk to the postbox and back after breakfast on Tuesday and Thursday" succeeds.

Small enough to be certain. The first assignment should be one the clinician is confident will be completed, because early success predicts continued engagement.

Explicitly linked to the mechanism. The client should be able to say why they are doing it.

Anticipate the obstacle before it happens.

> "What's most likely to stop this happening? ... And if the meeting overruns, what's the fallback?"

Reviewed first, always. The single strongest predictor of homework not being done is the clinician not asking about it. Reviewing it in the first five minutes of the next session establishes that it matters.

Non-completion is data, not misconduct. The response is curiosity, not disappointment.

> Poor: "That's okay, let's try again this week."
> Better: "Let's find out what happened, because that's usually the most useful thing in the week. Where did it break down: remembering, starting, or something coming up in the middle?"

Language matters. "Homework" carries school associations for many people, and "experiment", "practice", "between-session task" or the client's own name for it often works better.

## Recognising that the approach is not working

The most consequential clinical skill in this section is noticing failure early. Clinicians are unreliable at identifying clients who are deteriorating without formal measurement, and the research on routine outcome monitoring exists precisely because unaided clinical judgement misses a substantial proportion of deteriorating cases (Lambert and colleagues; Hannan et al., 2005) [UNVERIFIED as to specific detection rates].

The practical answer is a session-by-session measure. PHQ-9, GAD-7, CORE-10, ORS or an equivalent, taken every session, plotted, and looked at. Measurement without inspection is theatre.

Signals that the approach is wrong.

No movement on the measure after an adequate dose, typically six to eight sessions of a working alliance and completed tasks.

The client is engaged and compliant and nothing is changing. This points to the formulation rather than to motivation.

Sessions have become pleasant and directionless. The clinician looks forward to them and cannot say what is being worked on.

The same content recurs weekly with no development. Repetition without deepening is a marker of a maintenance conversation.

Deterioration, including new symptoms, increased risk, or increased substance use.

Increasing cancellations, lateness or shortened engagement, which is often a withdrawal rupture rather than lack of motivation.

The clinician is working harder than the client, session after session.

Common causes and what they indicate. A missed diagnosis, including bipolarity, autism, ADHD, an eating disorder, a physical cause such as thyroid disease or sleep apnoea, or substance dependence. An unaddressed maintaining factor outside the room, such as ongoing abuse, debt or an unsafe workplace. A wrong mechanism in the formulation. An unrepaired rupture. Insufficient dose or intensity. A treatment that requires a specialist competence the clinician does not have.

## Changing course

Noticing is not enough. The correction has a sequence.

Name it openly. Concealing a stalled therapy from the client is a common and corrosive error, and clients almost always already know.

> "I want to be straight with you. We're eight sessions in, your scores haven't moved, and I don't think what we're doing is working. That's mine to solve, not yours, and I'd like to look at it with you."

Ask the client for their account before offering yours. Clients frequently name the reason immediately when they are asked directly.

> "What's your read on why this hasn't shifted? And what have you been thinking but not saying?"

Revisit the formulation rather than intensifying the same technique. Doing more of a treatment that is not working is the standard error. Check for a missed maintaining factor, a missed diagnosis, and a mismatch between the mechanism and the method.

Consider the alternatives in order: change the target within the same model; change the model; add a component such as medication, family work or a practical intervention; refer for a specialist treatment; refer for assessment of a suspected missed condition; or pause the therapy.

Pausing is a legitimate clinical decision. Continuing indefinitely with an ineffective therapy has costs: it occupies a slot, it consumes the client's hope, and it teaches them that treatment does not work for them.

> "One option I want to put on the table is stopping for now. Not because you've failed, and not permanently. If we keep going out of politeness we'll use up something that might work better in six months when the housing situation is settled."

Take it to supervision or consultation before deciding. A stalled case is the paradigm indication for consultation, and in DBT the consultation team exists partly for this function.

## Endings, stepping down and relapse prevention

Endings are part of the treatment and are planned rather than arrived at. Time-limited work should have the ending named at the start and referred to periodically, not mentioned for the first time in the penultimate session.

Content of a good ending. Review of what changed and how, with the client naming the mechanism in their own words, since attributing change to their own action rather than to the clinician predicts maintenance. A written or spoken relapse prevention plan: early warning signs specific to this person, what to do at each stage, who to contact and how. A blueprint of the techniques that worked. Explicit discussion of the relationship ending, which for people with attachment injuries or bereavement histories is live material rather than administrative.

> "What would be the first sign, three or four weeks before you were properly unwell again, that you'd notice and someone else might not?"

> "If this comes back in eighteen months, what do you want your future self to do in the first week?"

Stepping down rather than stopping is often the right structure: reducing to fortnightly, then monthly, then a single review appointment at three months. Booster sessions are a standard feature of relapse prevention in depression and are underused.

Endings that go wrong. Fading out through cancellations without a final session, which leaves the work unconsolidated and repeats an abandonment pattern for many clients. Extending indefinitely because ending is uncomfortable for either party. Ending abruptly at a service boundary with no notice, which is a service failure and should be named as such to the client rather than absorbed.

> "I want to say clearly that ending here is about the service's limit, not about you being finished or not deserving more. I'm sorry about it, and I'd rather say that than pretend it's clinically ideal."
