## Standard cognitive therapy (Beck): the model and what it claims to change

Beck's cognitive model holds that emotional distress is maintained by the appraisal of events rather than the events themselves (Beck, 1976; Beck, Rush, Shaw and Emery, 1979). The model is layered. Automatic thoughts sit at the surface and are situation-specific. Underlying assumptions or conditional rules sit beneath them, usually phrased as if-then or must statements. Core beliefs sit at the base and are absolute statements about self, others and the world. The claimed change mechanism is that a person learns to treat thoughts as hypotheses rather than facts, tests them against evidence and experience, and thereby reduces the frequency and credibility of the appraisals that drive symptoms.

Structurally, a Beckian session has a recognisable shape: mood check with a brief measure, bridge from the last session, agenda setting done collaboratively, review of the last homework, one or two agenda items worked in depth, new homework set collaboratively, and a summary plus feedback request at the end (Beck, 2011). The structure is not decoration. It is what allows a time-limited therapy to cover a formulation, a skill and a between-session task in fifty minutes.

The case formulation is the organising document. It maps early experience, the core beliefs formed from it, the conditional assumptions that keep those beliefs from being disconfirmed, the compensatory strategies the person uses, and the critical incident that broke the strategy. A clinician who is doing cognitive therapy well can say at any moment which part of the formulation the current technique is targeting.

Evidence: CBT is recommended by NICE as a treatment option across depression severities in adults (NICE guideline NG222, 2022), for OCD and body dysmorphic disorder including exposure and response prevention (NICE guideline CG31, 2005), and as trauma-focused CBT for PTSD presenting more than one month after the event (NICE guideline NG116, 2018). CBT for psychosis is recommended by NICE and should be delivered over at least sixteen planned sessions (NICE guideline CG178, 2014).

The most common failure in standard CBT is running the technique without the formulation. A thought record produced because it is week three of a protocol, rather than because this person's distress is maintained by a specific appraisal, produces compliance and no change. The second most common failure is treating cognitive restructuring as persuasion. If the clinician knows the "right" answer to the thought record before the client does, the exercise has become a debate, and the client will typically report the intellectual answer while retaining the emotional belief. The clinician's marker for this is the client saying some version of "I know it rationally but I don't feel it."

> "Can I check the shape of what we're doing? Last week we agreed the thing that keeps this going is the thought that if you ask for help you'll be seen as incompetent. Today I want to test that directly rather than talk about it. Does that fit with what you'd hoped to use the time for, or is something more pressing?"

## Cognitive restructuring: the actual sequence

Cognitive restructuring is a sequence, not a single move. The order matters because each step depends on the previous one being concrete.

First, catch a specific moment. The clinician resists working with a generalisation and pulls for an episode with a time and place attached. Second, identify the emotion and rate its intensity, which anchors change to something measurable. Third, identify the hot thought, meaning the cognition carrying the most affective charge, not simply the first thought reported. Fourth, rate belief in the hot thought as a percentage. Fifth, gather evidence for and against, with the "for" column collected first and taken seriously. Sixth, construct an alternative or balanced view in the client's own words. Seventh, re-rate emotion and belief.

The downward arrow technique is the standard route from an automatic thought to a core belief. The clinician repeatedly asks what it would mean if the thought were true, without challenging anything, until the client arrives at an absolute statement.

> Client: "I think they noticed I went blank in the meeting."
> Clinician: "Let's say they did notice. What would that mean to you?"
> Client: "That they'd think I wasn't on top of the project."
> Clinician: "And if they thought that?"
> Client: "They'd start checking my work."
> Clinician: "And if they started checking your work, what would that say?"
> Client: "That I'm not actually capable of this job. That I got here by luck."

Behavioural experiments are usually more potent than verbal disputation, and the field has moved toward them. The design has four parts: the specific prediction with a probability attached, the experiment that could disconfirm it, the actual result, and the revised belief. The prediction must be falsifiable. "It will go badly" is not usable. "At least three people will look away when I speak, and I will not be able to finish the sentence" is.

> "Before we go anywhere near whether that's true, I want to know exactly what you're predicting. If you send that email without rewriting it four times, what specifically happens? What percentage would you put on it?"

Common misapplications: challenging a thought that is accurate. If a client believes their partner is critical of them and the partner is in fact critical, the target is not the appraisal but the situation or the meaning attached to it. Restructuring an accurate perception is gaslighting with a worksheet. A second misapplication is restructuring thoughts about a live danger, such as a client in an abusive relationship appraising risk correctly. A third is using restructuring where the presenting mechanism is avoidance, where the exposure or activation work carries the change and cognitive work should follow it rather than precede it.

## Socratic questioning: the four-stage sequence with verbatim wording

Socratic questioning is guided discovery, not cross-examination. Padesky's (1993) formulation gives four stages that repeat in cycles: ask informational questions the client can answer, listen empathically, summarise what has emerged, then ask a synthesising or analytical question that applies the new information to the original belief. Skipping the third stage is the most common error, because without the summary the client does not experience the new material as a coherent set.

Therapist use of Socratic questioning, rated by observers in the first three sessions of a sixteen-week course of cognitive therapy for depression, predicted session-to-session symptom change: a one standard deviation increase in Socratic questioning predicted a 1.51-point decrease in BDI-II score in the following session, and the effect held after controlling for the therapeutic alliance (Braun, Strunk, Sasso and Cooper, 2015).

A worked sequence on the belief "I'm a burden to my friends":

> Clinician (informational): "When you think about being a burden, whose face comes to mind first?"
> Client: "Sam."
> Clinician (informational): "When did you last see Sam?"
> Client: "Saturday. He asked me to come over."
> Clinician (informational): "Who made the arrangement?"
> Client: "He texted me. He'd asked twice before that too."
> Clinician (empathic listening): "So it sounds like being around him has felt like something you're imposing, even while he's the one initiating."
> Client: "Yeah. I keep thinking he's being polite."
> Clinician (summary): "Let me put together what we've got. You believe you're a burden. When we look at the most recent evidence, Sam initiated three times, you didn't ask once, and your explanation for that is that he's being polite."
> Clinician (analytical): "If a friend of yours told you they'd invited someone over three times out of politeness, what would you make of that?"

Note what the clinician does not do: assert that the client is not a burden, supply the reframe, or ask a leading question with the answer embedded. The last question is the analytical one, and the client generates the conclusion.

Rules for the wording. Ask questions the client has the information to answer. Do not ask questions to which the clinician already knows the answer and the client does not, because that is teaching disguised as enquiry. Ask about specifics before generalities. Use "what" and "how" more than "why", because "why" invites justification and often produces defensiveness. When the client generates an answer that goes against the clinician's hypothesis, follow the client's answer rather than repeating the question in a different form.

Failure modes to recognise in transcript. Chained rhetorical questions with an obvious intended answer ("Do you really think that's fair on yourself?"). Questions that function as reassurance ("But you know you're doing well, don't you?"). Rapid questioning without pauses, which produces compliance rather than discovery. If the client is answering in three words and the clinician is speaking in paragraphs, the sequence has failed.

## Behavioural activation: mechanism, technique, evidence

Behavioural activation treats depression as maintained by a loss of contact with positive reinforcement, compounded by avoidance and withdrawal that reduce it further (Lewinsohn's behavioural model; Martell, Dimidjian and Herman-Dunn, 2010). The claimed mechanism is that scheduled, graded increases in activity restore contact with reinforcement, and that this happens without requiring any change in thought content. BA is deliberately not a cognitive therapy: it treats rumination as a behaviour to be interrupted rather than a set of propositions to be tested.

Core techniques: activity monitoring to establish a baseline of what the person actually does and how they feel doing it; activity scheduling using values or life areas rather than pleasant events lists alone; graded task assignment breaking activities down until the first step is small enough to be certain; functional analysis of avoidance, usually taught to clients as TRAP (Trigger, Response, Avoidance Pattern) and TRAC (Trigger, Response, Alternative Coping); and attention to acting according to a plan rather than according to mood.

Evidence: a component analysis found that behavioural activation alone performed comparably to the full cognitive-behavioural package for depression (Jacobson et al., 1996). In a randomised placebo-controlled trial of 241 adults with major depression, among the more severely depressed participants behavioural activation performed comparably to antidepressant medication and both outperformed cognitive therapy (Dimidjian et al., 2006). The COBRA trial found behavioural activation delivered by junior mental health workers non-inferior to CBT delivered by qualified therapists, with a mean difference of 0.1 PHQ-9 points (95% CI -1.3 to 1.5), and lower cost (Richards et al., 2016, Lancet). NICE lists behavioural activation among the recommended options for less severe and more severe depression in adults (NICE guideline NG222, 2022).

The central verbal move in BA is outside-in rather than inside-out.

> "I want to be honest about the order of operations here. We are not going to wait until you feel like doing things and then do them. We're going to do them at the times we plan, and check what happens to the mood afterwards. The feeling tends to follow the action, and it usually arrives late."

On rumination as behaviour:

> "When you were sitting on the bed going over the conversation, what was that doing for you in the moment? I don't mean was it useful in the long run. I mean what did it get you right then."

Contraindications and misapplications. BA fails when the schedule is set from the clinician's idea of a good life rather than the client's values, producing a list of chores. It fails when the first step is too large, because a failed assignment confirms the depressive prediction. It fails when the clinician does not do the functional analysis and so schedules activities that the client is avoiding for a good reason, such as contact with an abusive family member. In severe depression with marked psychomotor retardation, the initial steps have to be very small, sometimes at the level of sitting up or opening a curtain, and setting normal-sized tasks at this stage is an error rather than an ambition.

## Exposure and response prevention: habituation versus inhibitory learning

Exposure treats anxiety disorders and OCD as maintained by avoidance and by safety behaviours that prevent disconfirmation. The older account of the mechanism was habituation: sustained contact with the feared stimulus produces a decline in anxiety within and between sessions, and this decline is the therapeutic event. The current account is inhibitory learning: extinction does not erase the original fear association but creates a new, competing association, and the strength of the new learning depends on the degree to which the outcome violates the person's expectation (Craske, Treanor, Conway, Zbozinek and Vervliet, 2014). On this account within-session fear reduction is not the target and is a poor predictor of outcome.

The practical consequences of the shift are large. Under an inhibitory learning model the clinician elicits a specific, falsifiable prediction before the exposure, designs the exposure to maximise the discrepancy between prediction and outcome, removes safety behaviours because they block disconfirmation, varies context and stimulus rather than repeating an identical task, and does not use anxiety ratings as a stopping rule. Craske et al. (2014) list strategies including expectancy violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction, removal of safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple contexts, reconsolidation, and affect labelling.

Response prevention is the component that distinguishes ERP for OCD from exposure alone. The client contacts the trigger and does not perform the compulsion, including mental compulsions such as reviewing, counting, praying to neutralise, or seeking reassurance. Reassurance-seeking from the clinician is itself a compulsion and has to be named and blocked, which is one of the harder moments in the work.

Evidence: in a randomised placebo-controlled trial, intensive exposure and ritual prevention produced treated and completer response rates of 62% and 86%, compared with 42% and 48% for clomipramine and 8% and 10% for placebo (Foa et al., 2005, American Journal of Psychiatry). NICE recommends CBT including exposure and response prevention across severity levels for OCD (NICE guideline CG31, 2005). For PTSD, prolonged exposure and cognitive processing therapy carry recommendations in the 2023 VA/DoD clinical practice guideline alongside EMDR, and the guideline recommends manualised trauma-focused psychotherapies over pharmacotherapy.

Contraindications and misapplications. Exposure delivered without an explicit rationale that the client has bought into becomes coercion and produces dropout. Exposure conducted while the client uses covert safety behaviours produces no learning and teaches the client that exposure does not work. Exposure to a real current danger is not exposure, it is a risk. In active self-harm, unmanaged substance dependence, or acute suicidality, stabilisation takes precedence. Exposure where the feared outcome is a genuine possibility, such as a health-anxious client with an actual undiagnosed symptom, requires medical clarity first. Finally, the very common misapplication of asking a client to "sit with it until the anxiety halves" is a habituation-era instruction, and under the current model the more defensible instruction is to stay long enough to find out whether the prediction was right.

## Building and running an exposure hierarchy: verbatim wording

Setting up:

> "I want to be straight with you about what this involves, because I don't want you to agree to something you haven't seen the shape of. We are going to deliberately approach the things you've been avoiding, in an order you choose, and while we do that you'll drop the things you normally do to keep the anxiety down. It will be uncomfortable. It will not be a surprise. You'll always know what we're doing before we do it, and you can stop. What I won't do is talk you into feeling calm at the moment you're most anxious, because that's the part that keeps this going."

Eliciting the prediction:

> "Before you touch the handle, tell me what you think will happen. Be as specific as you can. Not 'something bad', but what, to whom, and how soon. And put a number on it. Out of a hundred, how likely?"

During:

> "You said seventy per cent that you'd be contaminated and that it would spread to your daughter by tonight. We're at eight minutes. What's your number for that prediction now?"

Blocking a compulsion, including the reassurance version:

> "You just asked me whether it's safe. I'm not going to answer that, and I want to say why rather than just refusing. If I answer, the relief you get will belong to me rather than to you, and the question will come back tomorrow. What I will do is stay here while you find out."

After:

> "What did you predict, and what actually happened? Not how anxious you felt. What happened."

Handling a client whose feared outcome cannot be disconfirmed within the session, such as a feared illness with a long latency:

> "We can't settle whether you'll get ill in the next ten years, and I'm not going to pretend we can. What we can test is whether you can hold the uncertainty for an hour without checking. That's the thing that's actually costing you."

## Acceptance and commitment therapy: the hexaflex and the moves it names

ACT (Hayes, Strosahl and Wilson, 1999; 2012) targets psychological flexibility rather than symptom reduction. The claimed mechanism is that suffering is amplified by experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion, and that living in accordance with chosen values while making room for unwanted internal experience produces improved functioning even where symptom frequency does not change. Symptom reduction is treated as a possible by-product, not the goal, which is a genuine difference from standard CBT rather than a presentational one.

The hexaflex names six processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values, and committed action. The first four are the mindfulness and acceptance side, the last four, overlapping in the middle, are the commitment and behaviour change side.

Acceptance is willingness to have the internal experience without struggling against it. The clinical move is to shift the question from how to reduce the feeling to what the struggle costs.

> "How long have you been fighting the anxiety? And in that time, has it got smaller? I'm not asking to score a point. I'm asking whether the strategy has delivered, because if it has we should do more of it."

Defusion changes the relationship to a thought rather than its content. Techniques include prefacing ("I'm having the thought that..."), repetition of a word until it loses meaning, naming the mind as a separate speaker, and thanking the mind.

> "Try saying it as 'I'm noticing I'm having the thought that I'm a failure.' Say the two versions back to back and tell me if anything shifts in how much it grips."

Contact with the present moment is attentional flexibility, usually trained with brief in-session exercises rather than long meditations.

Self-as-context separates the observing self from the content observed.

> "You've been anxious, and calm, and furious, and bored, all in the last week. Who has been there for all of them?"

Values are chosen life directions, not goals. The clinician distinguishes them explicitly, because clients import goals.

> "Getting the promotion is a goal. You can succeed or fail at it, and then it's over. What I'm asking about is the direction underneath it. If the promotion never came, what would still be worth doing about the kind of colleague you want to be?"

Committed action is the behavioural half and looks similar to graded task assignment, with the difference that the assignment is linked to a value and the willingness to carry discomfort is made explicit.

> "So the move is to say the thing in Thursday's meeting. And the price of admission is that your chest will be tight and your voice may shake. Are you willing to take those along, if that's what it costs to speak up about work you care about?"

The creative hopelessness sequence opens many ACT courses. It is an inventory of everything the client has tried to control the internal experience, followed by a shared assessment of what it has cost. It is done with warmth or not at all, because delivered coldly it lands as an accusation of failure.

## ACT evidence, limits and misapplications

Evidence: a meta-analysis of 39 randomised controlled trials with 1,821 patients found ACT superior to control conditions with Hedges' g = 0.57 at post-treatment, and specifically g = 0.82 versus waitlist, 0.51 versus psychological placebo, and 0.64 versus treatment as usual (A-Tjak et al., 2015, Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics). A more sceptical review of 60 RCTs with 4,234 participants found a mean effect size of 0.42 across comparisons and a small, non-significant effect of 0.16 when ACT was compared with other cognitive or behavioural treatments, and concluded ACT was not yet well established for any disorder, probably efficacious for chronic pain and tinnitus, and possibly efficacious for depression, psychotic symptoms, OCD, mixed anxiety, drug abuse and work stress (Öst, 2014, Behaviour Research and Therapy). Both should be held at once: ACT clearly beats doing nothing, and the case that it beats CBT specifically is weak.

Misapplications. The largest is using acceptance language to talk a client out of changing a changeable situation. Acceptance is for internal experience, not for circumstances that can and should be altered. Telling someone in an unsafe workplace or a violent relationship to make room for the feeling and move toward their values is a serious clinical error.

The second is using defusion as suppression. "Just notice the thought and let it go" delivered as an instruction to stop having the thought is fusion in acceptance costume, and clients detect it.

The third is values work conducted before the person has any behavioural traction, which can produce a vivid picture of a life they are not living and a sharp increase in shame.

The fourth is ACT used to avoid exposure. Where a specific phobia or OCD indicates ERP, running values and defusion work instead is a downgrade in the strength of the intervention, and the clinician should know that is the trade being made.

## Dialectical behaviour therapy: structure, stance and evidence

DBT (Linehan, 1993; Linehan, 2015) was developed for chronically suicidal and self-harming women meeting criteria for borderline personality disorder. The theory is biosocial: an emotionally vulnerable temperament transacting over time with an invalidating environment produces pervasive emotion dysregulation, and the behaviours that bring people to services are largely attempts to regulate or escape unbearable affect.

Standard DBT is a programme, not a technique. It has four components: individual therapy, skills group, telephone coaching between sessions, and a therapist consultation team. The consultation team is a treatment component, not supervision as an optional extra, and a service delivering the first three without the fourth is not delivering standard DBT.

Treatment is organised by a target hierarchy applied in order within each session: life-threatening behaviours first, then therapy-interfering behaviours (by either party), then quality-of-life-interfering behaviours, then skills acquisition. Diary cards track targets between sessions and set the agenda.

The four skills modules are mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness. Distress tolerance skills are explicitly crisis survival tools that do not solve the problem, and clients should be told so rather than led to expect resolution.

The dialectical stance is the balance between acceptance and change, held simultaneously rather than alternated.

> "Two things are both true. You did the best you could with what you had on Tuesday. And Tuesday cannot happen again, and we have to build something different. I'm not going to give up either half of that."

Evidence: in a two-year randomised controlled trial of 101 women with recent suicidal and self-injurious behaviour, DBT produced better outcomes than community treatment by non-behavioural experts across most target areas, and DBT participants were half as likely to make a suicide attempt (Linehan et al., 2006, Archives of General Psychiatry). NICE recommends that a comprehensive DBT programme be considered for women with borderline personality disorder for whom reducing recurrent self-harm is a priority, and advises against brief psychological interventions of less than three months' duration for borderline personality disorder (NICE guideline CG78, 2009).

Misapplications. "DBT skills" delivered as a psychoeducation group with no individual therapy, no coaching and no consultation team is a common service adaptation, and it should be described as skills training rather than DBT. Validation used without any change demand produces a warm therapy that does not move. Change demand without validation produces dropout, and in this population dropout has consequences. Diary cards used as compliance monitoring rather than as data for chain analysis convert the client into a defendant.

## DBT chain analysis: the full sequence with wording

Behavioural chain analysis is a minute-by-minute reconstruction of a single target behaviour, done to locate the specific points where a different response was possible. It is done on one episode, not on a pattern, and specificity is the whole value of it.

The components in order: the target behaviour defined precisely; vulnerability factors present beforehand; the prompting event, which should be something a camera could have recorded; each link between prompt and behaviour, broken into thoughts, emotions, body sensations, urges and actions; the consequences, short-term and long-term, for the client and for others; then solution analysis, which installs an alternative response at specific links; then a prevention plan for the vulnerability factors, repair of any damage done, and a concrete commitment.

> Clinician: "Let's start at the behaviour and work backwards. On Thursday, what exactly did you do?"
> Client: "I cut."
> Clinician: "Where, when, with what, and for how long? I know this is unpleasant to go over in detail. The detail is where the handholds are."
> Client: "Bathroom, about eleven at night, the blade from the pencil sharpener. Maybe five minutes."
> Clinician: "What was going on before Thursday that made you more vulnerable? Sleep, food, illness, alcohol, anything left over from earlier in the week?"
> Client: "I hadn't slept properly since Monday and I'd skipped lunch two days running."
> Clinician: "So we start Thursday already low on resources. Now the prompting event. What was the first thing that happened, the thing a camera would have caught?"
> Client: "My sister's message came through at nine forty. It said 'we'll talk about it when you're less dramatic.'"
> Clinician: "Nine forty, message arrives. What's the very next thing, in your body or your head?"

The clinician continues link by link, resisting summaries and jumps. When the client says "and then I just did it", the clinician slows down rather than accepting the compression:

> "I want to slow down right there, because 'I just did it' has about ten minutes inside it. You put the phone down. What happened next?"

Solution analysis:

> "We've got seven or eight places where the chain could have gone somewhere else. Let's take the one where you were standing in the kitchen with the urge at about a six. That's earlier than we usually catch it. What skill could have gone in right there? Not what should have. What actually could have, at a six, with no sleep."

Chain analysis is not punishment and should not feel like one. If the client experiences it as a tribunal, the therapy has drifted, and the correction is usually more validation earlier in the chain, not less detail.

## Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, compassion-focused therapy and metacognitive therapy

MBCT (Segal, Williams and Teasdale, 2002) was designed for relapse prevention in recurrent depression. The claimed mechanism is decentring: attention training in an eight-week group format lets a person recognise the early re-activation of depressive thinking and disengage from rumination before it escalates. Teasdale et al. (2000) found relapse prevention effects concentrated in participants with three or more previous episodes. The PREVENT trial randomised 424 patients to MBCT with antidepressant tapering support or to maintenance antidepressants and found no difference in time to relapse over 24 months (hazard ratio 0.89, 95% CI 0.67 to 1.18, p = 0.43), which supports MBCT as an alternative for people who want to come off medication rather than as a superior option (Kuyken et al., 2015, Lancet). MBCT is not designed as an acute treatment for a current severe episode, and running it as one is the standard misapplication. Intensive mindfulness practice can also destabilise people with dissociative presentations or acute trauma symptoms, and screening for that is part of group selection.

CFT (Gilbert, 2009, 2010) was developed for people whose central problem is shame and self-attack, and specifically for those who can generate an alternative thought but feel no relief from it. The model proposes three interacting affect regulation systems: threat and protection, drive and resource-seeking, and affiliation and soothing. The claimed mechanism is that the soothing system is underdeveloped in people with hostile or neglectful early environments, so cognitive change lands on threat-toned processing and does not soothe. Techniques include compassionate mind training, soothing rhythm breathing, imagery of a compassionate other or compassionate self, chair work between the self-critic and the criticised self, and letter writing from a compassionate perspective. A systematic review and meta-analysis of CFT with clinical populations reports benefits for self-criticism, shame and self-compassion, with the caveat that trials are generally small and heterogeneous (systematic review and meta-analysis of CFT effectiveness in clinical populations, Journal of Affective Disorders, 2023; the specific author list should be confirmed at the source before quoting) [UNVERIFIED as to authorship and pooled effect size]. A known clinical phenomenon is fear of compassion: some clients respond to compassionate imagery with grief, anger or increased threat, and this is expected in the model rather than a sign the technique failed.

Metacognitive therapy (Wells, 2009) derives from the self-regulatory executive function model (Wells and Matthews, 1994). The claimed mechanism is that psychopathology is maintained by the cognitive attentional syndrome, meaning perseverative worry and rumination, threat monitoring, and unhelpful coping, and that this is driven by metacognitive beliefs about thinking rather than by the content of thoughts. MCT therefore does not challenge the content of negative thoughts at all, which distinguishes it sharply from Beckian work. Techniques include the attention training technique, detached mindfulness, worry and rumination postponement, and direct challenge to positive metacognitive beliefs ("worrying keeps me prepared") and negative ones ("my worry is uncontrollable"). A meta-analysis of 25 trials covering 780 adults found MCT superior to waitlist and to active control conditions, with pooled Hedges' g of 0.69 against cognitive and behavioural interventions at post-treatment and 0.37 at follow-up (Normann and Morina, 2018, Frontiers in Psychology). The evidence base is smaller than for CBT and a substantial proportion of trials come from groups associated with the model's developer, which is a reason for measured claims.

> MCT wording: "I don't want to know whether the thought is true. I want to know what you believe would happen if you didn't engage with it at all. What's the worry doing for you?"

## Contraindications and failure modes across the CBT family

Some cautions apply across the whole family and should be checked before any protocol starts.

Acute risk overrides protocol. Active suicidal intent, unmanaged self-harm, or an unsafe living situation moves the work to risk management and stabilisation. Starting trauma processing or intensive exposure with a person who has no means of down-regulating afterwards, and no support until the next appointment, is the most consequential error in this family.

Unmanaged substance dependence undermines learning-based treatments, because the substance functions as the avoidance and blocks the new learning. This does not mean abstinence is a precondition for all work, but it does mean the clinician should know what the substance is doing in the maintenance cycle.

Cognitive load matters. Protocols with written homework, thought records and self-monitoring assume literacy, working memory and time. Acquired brain injury, active psychosis, severe depression with concentration impairment, and intellectual disability all require adaptation, and unadapted protocols produce a record of non-compliance that gets attributed to the client.

Homework non-completion is data, not a moral failure. The first hypothesis should be that the task was too large, insufficiently specific, or not connected to anything the client cares about. The second is that the rationale was not shared. The third is a rupture in the alliance that the client is expressing behaviourally rather than verbally.

Recognising that the model is wrong. If a client is completing tasks, engaging in session, and not improving after six to eight sessions, the formulation is the first suspect, not the client's motivation. Common formulation errors: treating a trauma presentation as an anxiety disorder, treating a bipolar depression as unipolar, missing an eating disorder that is doing the emotion regulation work, missing autism where the presenting anxiety is a consequence of unsupported sensory and social demand, and missing an ongoing situation such as bullying, debt, violence or insecure immigration status where the appropriate intervention is practical rather than psychological.

Protocol fidelity and responsiveness both matter, and clinicians err in both directions. Rigid adherence that ignores what happened in the client's week produces disengagement. Endless flexibility that never completes a technique produces a supportive relationship with no mechanism of change. The workable position is to keep the mechanism constant and vary the delivery.
