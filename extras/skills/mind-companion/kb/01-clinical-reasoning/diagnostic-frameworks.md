## What a diagnostic system is for, and the three jobs it does badly

A psychiatric classification is a communication technology. It exists so that clinicians, researchers, services and payers can refer to the same clinical picture using the same word, so that treatment research can be pooled, and so that epidemiology and service planning are possible. Judged against those aims it has partial success. Judged against the aims people commonly attribute to it, it fails.

The first thing it does badly is explain. A diagnosis of major depressive disorder does not tell you why the person is depressed, what triggered this episode, or what is maintaining it. The category is defined by a symptom count over a duration, not by a mechanism. Diagnosis names a pattern; it does not account for it. This is the central reason Macneil, Hasty, Conus and Berk (2012) argued that diagnosis alone is insufficient to guide intervention.

The second thing it does badly is carve nature at the joints. Comorbidity is the norm rather than the exception, which is what you would expect if the categories were cutting across underlying continuous dimensions rather than tracking discrete conditions. Within-category heterogeneity is extreme; two people can both meet criteria for major depressive disorder while sharing very few symptoms.

The third thing it does badly is achieve reliability under field conditions. The DSM-5 field trials reported test-retest kappas in the questionable range for two of the most common diagnoses, with major depressive disorder around 0.28 and generalised anxiety disorder around 0.20 (Regier et al., 2013). These were disorders chosen partly because they were relatively unchanged from DSM-IV and could serve as benchmarks. If two trained clinicians assessing the same patient days apart agree at that level, the label carries less information than its use implies.

What diagnosis does do well is more limited and more useful than its critics sometimes allow. It gives access to treatment, funding, benefits, accommodations and legal protections that in most systems are gated on codes. It gives clinicians a rapid heuristic for which evidence base to consult. It gives some people a name for their experience that reduces shame and connects them to others. It supports pharmacological decision-making, where the evidence base genuinely is organised by category.

The correct stance is that a diagnosis is a hypothesis about which pattern a presentation most resembles, with practical consequences attached, and not a discovered fact about the person. Everything that follows in this file should be read in that light.

## DSM-5-TR: structure and the logic of its organisation

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision, was published by the American Psychiatric Association in March 2022. It is organised in three sections. Section I covers use of the manual, including the definition of mental disorder and the cautionary statement on forensic use. Section II contains the diagnostic criteria and codes, grouped into diagnostic chapters. Section III contains emerging measures and models, including the Alternative Model for Personality Disorders, the Cultural Formulation Interview, and conditions for further study.

Section II's chapter ordering is not arbitrary. DSM-5 abandoned the multiaxial system of DSM-IV and reorganised the chapters along a developmental lifespan sequence, beginning with neurodevelopmental disorders that typically manifest early, moving through schizophrenia spectrum, bipolar, depressive, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive, trauma and stressor-related, dissociative, somatic symptom, feeding and eating, elimination, sleep-wake, sexual dysfunction, gender dysphoria, disruptive and impulse-control, substance-related, neurocognitive, personality, and paraphilic disorders. Adjacent chapters are intended to reflect suspected shared underlying vulnerability, so that bipolar disorders sit between the psychotic and depressive chapters because they share features with both.

The internal logic of a DSM criteria set is polythetic and operational. A polythetic set requires some number of symptoms from a longer list rather than requiring all of them, which is why two people can share a diagnosis with minimal symptom overlap. Criteria are operationalised as counts, durations and thresholds. Nearly every criteria set carries a clinical significance criterion requiring distress or functional impairment, and exclusion criteria requiring that the presentation is not better explained by another condition, substance use, or a medical condition. That exclusion clause is the formal instruction to conduct a differential.

DSM-5-TR retained the DSM-5 structure and criteria almost entirely. The substantive changes were the addition of prolonged grief disorder as a new diagnosis, requiring the death to have occurred at least twelve months earlier for adults and six months for children and adolescents, with persistent yearning or preoccupation plus three of eight symptoms present most days for at least the past month; new symptom codes allowing clinicians to record current or historical suicidal behaviour and non-suicidal self-injury independently of any diagnosis; extensive revision of the explanatory text including prevalence, risk factors, culture, sex and gender; and revised terminology for racial and ethnic descriptions.

The limits of the system are structural. Thresholds are largely conventional rather than empirically derived. The categories do not correspond to distinct aetiologies. Comorbidity rates suggest the boundaries are wrong. Cultural validity varies by category. None of this makes the manual useless, but a clinician who treats a DSM code as an explanation has mistaken a filing system for a theory.

## ICD-11: what differs and why it differs

The International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision, was approved by the World Health Assembly in 2019, with member state reporting based on it from January 2022. The mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders chapter is accompanied by the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements, which replaced the ICD-10 Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines. Reed et al. (2019) describe the revision as the most international, multilingual and participative revision process yet undertaken for a mental disorders classification.

The most consequential difference is philosophical. ICD-11 was explicitly designed for clinical utility and global applicability, including in low-resource settings without specialist mental health services, whereas DSM prioritises operational precision for research and North American practice. ICD-11 therefore uses flexible prototype-style descriptions of essential features rather than rigid symptom counts and duration thresholds. A clinician is asked to judge whether the presentation matches the described prototype, not to count to five out of nine. This trades some reliability for usability and for cross-cultural applicability.

Specific substantive differences matter for differential reasoning. ICD-11 separates post-traumatic stress disorder into two sibling diagnoses, PTSD and complex PTSD, with complex PTSD requiring the PTSD features plus disturbances in self-organisation covering affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbances in relationships. DSM-5-TR has no complex PTSD diagnosis and instead broadened PTSD itself. ICD-11 also narrowed PTSD to a tighter set of core features, so the two systems identify overlapping but non-identical populations.

ICD-11 abolished the categorical personality disorder subtypes entirely. Personality disorder is diagnosed once, rated for severity as mild, moderate or severe, and then optionally described using trait domain qualifiers of negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition and anankastia, plus a borderline pattern specifier. This is closer to the DSM-5 Section III Alternative Model than to DSM-5 Section II, and it is the clearest example of a mainstream system adopting a dimensional structure.

Other differences include a distinct category for gaming disorder, prolonged grief disorder in both systems with slightly different requirements, and a restructured grouping for obsessive-compulsive and related disorders including body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding, and olfactory reference disorder.

The practical consequence for reasoning is that the same person can attract different labels under the two systems, particularly for trauma presentations and personality difficulty. When a diagnosis is quoted, the system it came from is part of the information.

## The categorical and dimensional debate

The categorical model treats disorders as discrete kinds with boundaries, so that a person either has the condition or does not. The dimensional model treats psychopathology as continuous variation, so that the clinical question is how much of a trait or symptom dimension a person has, not whether they are in or out.

The evidence favours dimensions for most of psychopathology. Taxometric studies have generally failed to find natural cut points separating cases from non-cases for common conditions. Symptom severity distributions are continuous without a zone of rarity at the diagnostic threshold. Subthreshold presentations carry real impairment and real prognostic weight, which a categorical system discards. Genetic and neurobiological findings do not respect diagnostic boundaries, with the same variants associated with multiple categories. The high rates of comorbidity are more parsimoniously read as an artefact of drawing multiple category boundaries across a smaller number of underlying dimensions.

The arguments for retaining categories are practical rather than ontological. Clinical decisions are frequently binary: treat or do not treat, admit or do not admit, prescribe or do not prescribe. Categories are cognitively economical and communicate quickly. Health systems, insurers, courts and disability schemes run on codes. Treatment evidence has been generated within categories for fifty years and cannot be reindexed overnight.

The compromise position adopted by both major manuals is hybrid. DSM-5 retained categories in Section II while adding severity specifiers, cross-cutting symptom measures, and a dimensional alternative model for personality in Section III. ICD-11 went further by making personality disorder fully dimensional.

For reasoning about an individual, the most useful implications of the dimensional view are these. Do not treat a subthreshold presentation as nothing; the person's impairment is the relevant variable, not their symptom count. Do not treat a threshold crossing as a change in kind; someone who moves from four to five symptoms has not become a different sort of person. Expect co-occurrence and do not multiply diagnoses to account for it when a single underlying dimension explains the picture. Treat severity, chronicity and functional impact as more informative than category membership.

## HiTOP: the hierarchical dimensional alternative

The Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology is a quantitatively derived dimensional classification built from factor-analytic evidence about how symptoms actually covary, developed by a consortium led by Kotov, Krueger, Watson and colleagues (Kotov et al., 2017). It is a bottom-up structure derived from data rather than a committee-defined taxonomy.

The hierarchy runs from narrow to broad. At the base sit signs, symptoms and maladaptive traits, the narrowest coherent dimensions. Above these sit syndromes, roughly corresponding to familiar DSM categories. Above these sit subfactors, small clusters of related syndromes such as distress or fear. Above these sit spectra. Above these sit superspectra. At the apex sits a general psychopathology factor.

Ruggero et al. (2019) identify six spectra: internalising, antagonistic externalising, disinhibited externalising, thought disorder, detachment, and somatoform. The internalising spectrum contains distress, fear, eating and sexual problem subfactors. The psychosis superspectrum has been the subject of dedicated validity work (Kotov et al., 2020).

The practical advantage is that a HiTOP profile describes a person on several continuous dimensions at once, which captures both severity and shape without the information loss of a threshold. It handles comorbidity natively, because co-occurrence is a consequence of shared higher-order variance rather than an anomaly requiring extra diagnoses. It captures subthreshold impairment. It aligns better than DSM categories with genetic and longitudinal findings.

The limits are frankly acknowledged by its own proponents. Ruggero et al. (2019) note that there is no direct evidence that using HiTOP dimensions improves treatment outcomes, that dimensional assessment adds administration and scoring burden, that reimbursement systems require ICD codes rather than dimensional profiles, that validated decision thresholds are underdeveloped, and that clinician training remains organised around categorical systems. The structure also continues to change as new data arrive, which is a scientific virtue and a clinical inconvenience.

Treat HiTOP as the best current description of the structure of psychopathology and as a corrective to over-interpretation of categories, while recognising that it does not yet function as a standalone clinical system.

## RDoC: a research framework, not a diagnostic system

The Research Domain Criteria framework was introduced by the National Institute of Mental Health in 2010 to reorient psychopathology research away from DSM categories and toward dimensions of observable behaviour and neurobiological measures (Insel et al., 2010). It is explicitly a research framework and was never intended for clinical diagnosis.

RDoC is organised as a matrix. The rows are domains of functioning and their constituent constructs. The original domains were negative valence systems, positive valence systems, cognitive systems, systems for social processes, and arousal and regulatory systems, with a sensorimotor systems domain added later. The columns are units of analysis running from genes, molecules, cells, circuits and physiology up to behaviour, self-report and paradigms. Developmental trajectory and environment are treated as cross-cutting considerations rather than as cells in the matrix.

The core commitment is that a research question should be framed in terms of a dimensional construct such as reward learning or threat responsivity, studied across the full range from normal to impaired, without recruiting on the basis of a DSM diagnosis. The intended payoff is a set of mechanistic constructs that eventually map onto treatment targets more usefully than syndrome labels do.

The value for clinical reasoning is conceptual rather than procedural. RDoC constructs give a vocabulary for what may actually be going wrong for a person: blunted reward responsivity, elevated acute threat response, impaired working memory, disrupted arousal regulation, poor social communication reception. These describe mechanisms and cut across categories, which is closer to what a formulation needs than a diagnostic label is.

The limits are substantial. RDoC has been criticised for biological reductionism, for underweighting social and environmental causation despite the environment being nominally a cross-cutting factor, and for failing after more than a decade to deliver the clinical translation that motivated it. Commentary on the framework's own trajectory has described it as becoming humbler about those original ambitions. It has no diagnostic algorithm, no thresholds, and no direct clinical application. Do not use RDoC constructs as if they were diagnoses.

## The p factor and what a general factor does and does not mean

Caspi and colleagues analysed eleven forms of psychopathology in a longitudinal birth cohort and found that a hierarchical model with three higher-order factors, internalising, externalising and thought disorder, fitted the data, but that a single general factor accounted for the covariation better still (Caspi et al., 2014). They named it p by analogy with g in the intelligence literature. The p factor was associated with greater life impairment, worse developmental histories, more compromised early-life brain function and family psychiatric loading.

The finding has been widely replicated across samples, ages and measurement approaches, and a general factor is now part of the HiTOP structure at its apex. The substantive claim is that people differ in a general liability to develop psychopathology of any kind, and that this general liability explains much of what looks like comorbidity between specific conditions.

Interpretation is contested. One reading is that p indexes a real dispositional vulnerability, possibly related to emotion dysregulation, negative emotionality, executive dysfunction, or disordered thought. A second reading is that p is largely a methodological artefact of how symptoms are measured, reflecting shared method variance, response style, or the fact that impairment and distress items appear across many scales. A third reading is that p indexes severity and chronicity rather than any distinct construct.

For clinical reasoning the useful implication is not the metaphysics but the prediction. If general liability is high, expect breadth rather than specificity: expect the person to have had several different presentations over time, expect symptoms outside the current diagnostic category, expect co-occurrence, and expect a worse course. Do not treat the appearance of a new symptom cluster as necessarily a new illness. High p should shift you toward transdiagnostic mechanisms and away from chasing each successive syndrome with a separate protocol.

## Transdiagnostic processes: repetitive negative thinking

Harvey, Watkins, Mansell and Shafran (2004) reviewed cognitive and behavioural processes across the disorders and argued that a set of common processes maintain symptoms across diagnostic boundaries. They examined attention, memory, reasoning, thought and behaviour, and asked of each whether it functions transdiagnostically. Their conclusion reframed maintenance: what keeps disorders going is often the same handful of processes applied to different content.

Repetitive negative thinking is the most researched of these. Ehring and Watkins (2008) defined it as a style of thinking that is repetitive, passive or relatively uncontrollable, and focused on negative content, and argued that worry, depressive rumination, post-event processing in social anxiety, and trauma-related rumination are the same process applied to disorder-specific content rather than distinct phenomena. Direct comparisons between worry and rumination have generally found more similarity than difference. Elevated repetitive negative thinking appears across a wide range of conditions and prospective evidence supports a causal role in maintaining emotional problems.

The clinical implications are direct. Ask about the process, not only the content. The relevant questions are how long the thinking goes on, whether it is experienced as controllable, whether it is abstract and evaluative or concrete and problem-solving, and whether it produces any action. Watkins' distinction between abstract evaluative processing, which maintains distress, and concrete process-focused thinking, which does not, is the operative target. Rumination-focused interventions target the style directly rather than disputing the content.

Assessing it requires probes aimed at form rather than content. How long does the thinking go on once it starts. Can you stop it if you try, and what happens when you try. Does it feel useful while you are doing it, and does it produce any decision or action. Is the thinking about specific concrete detail, such as what exactly happened and what you would do differently at a particular moment, or is it abstract and evaluative, such as why this always happens to you and what it says about you. The abstract evaluative mode is the one that sustains distress.

Two further points bear on formulation. Repetitive negative thinking is frequently valued by the person doing it, who experiences worry as preparation and rumination as problem-solving, so an intervention that treats it purely as a symptom to be eliminated will meet resistance that is rational from the person's perspective. It also interacts with sleep, since the thinking commonly occupies the pre-sleep period, which means that sleep disruption and rumination form a reinforcing loop that neither sleep hygiene nor cognitive work alone will break.

For differential reasoning, the presence of heavy repetitive negative thinking is diagnostically non-specific. It should raise the probability that whatever category you land on will not be the whole story, and it should raise the priority of treating the process itself.

## Transdiagnostic processes: experiential avoidance, emotion dysregulation, intolerance of uncertainty

Experiential avoidance was proposed by Hayes, Wilson, Gifford, Follette and Strosahl (1996) as a functional diagnostic dimension, defined as the attempt to alter the form, frequency or situational sensitivity of unwanted private experiences including thoughts, feelings, memories and bodily sensations, even when doing so causes behavioural harm. The paper's argument is that a wide range of presentations can be understood as unhealthy efforts to escape or avoid internal experience, and that classifying by function rather than by topography cuts across the diagnostic categories in a way that has treatment implications.

The clinical marker is not that the person avoids, since everyone does, but that avoidance has narrowed their life. The diagnostic question is what the person is unwilling to feel, and what they have given up in order not to feel it. Substance use, dissociation, compulsive behaviour, self-harm, workaholism, chronic distraction and interpersonal withdrawal can all serve this function. Two people with the same avoidance function and entirely different topographies require the same intervention logic.

Emotion dysregulation is the second major transdiagnostic process. Gross's process model of emotion regulation distinguishes strategies by where they act in the emotion-generative sequence: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation. Aldao, Nolen-Hoeksema and Schweizer (2010) meta-analysed the relationships between reappraisal, problem solving, thought suppression and rumination and symptoms of depression, anxiety, eating disorders and substance use. Maladaptive strategies, particularly rumination and suppression, showed consistently stronger associations with symptoms than adaptive strategies showed protective associations. This asymmetry matters clinically: removing a maladaptive strategy tends to buy more than adding an adaptive one.

Intolerance of uncertainty was developed within the generalised anxiety literature by Dugas and colleagues and is defined as a dispositional tendency to perceive uncertain situations as threatening, unfair or unacceptable. Carleton has argued that fear of the unknown may be the fundamental fear underlying anxiety and neuroticism more broadly (Carleton, 2016). Intolerance of uncertainty accounts for significant variance in panic disorder, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression and eating disorders, and it is now treated as a well-established transdiagnostic risk factor and treatment target.

The practical use of these three constructs is that they specify what to target when the diagnostic picture is mixed. A presentation featuring heavy experiential avoidance, poor emotion regulation and high intolerance of uncertainty will respond to work on those processes regardless of which categories it satisfies.

## The procedure of differential reasoning

Differential reasoning asks, for a given presentation, what else this could be and what would distinguish the alternatives. It is a discipline of generating rivals and then seeking evidence that separates them, and it is the single most important guard against premature closure.

The procedure has six steps.

First, describe the presentation at the level of phenomena rather than labels. Not "depression" but "reduced energy, loss of interest, early waking, weight loss of six kilograms over three months, and difficulty concentrating".

Second, establish the timeline. When did each element start, in what order, and what is the course: continuous, episodic, progressive, fluctuating, or lifelong? Timeline is the single most discriminating piece of information in psychiatric differential reasoning, and it is the piece most often omitted. Lifelong and pervasive points toward neurodevelopmental or personality-level explanation. Episodic with clear return to baseline points toward mood disorder. Onset following a specific event points toward stressor-related explanation. Progressive decline points toward neurocognitive or medical explanation.

Third, generate at least three candidate explanations, of which at least one must be non-psychiatric. The obligatory candidates for almost any presentation are a medical or organic cause, a substance or medication effect, a sleep disorder, and a normal response to abnormal circumstances.

Fourth, for each candidate, state what evidence would confirm it and what evidence would rule it out. This is the step that distinguishes reasoning from pattern-matching. If you cannot say what would rule a candidate out, you are not testing it.

Fifth, weight by base rates. Common conditions are common. In a general adult population, depression, anxiety, alcohol misuse, trauma sequelae, sleep deprivation and undiagnosed ADHD or autism are far more prevalent than the rarer conditions that a striking presentation may suggest.

Sixth, hold the conclusion provisionally and specify what would revise it. Write down the disconfirming observation you would expect to see if you are wrong.

## Worked differentials: four common presentations

Low mood, fatigue and loss of interest. Candidates: major depressive episode; bipolar depression, which requires screening for past hypomania because antidepressant monotherapy is contraindicated; hypothyroidism; iron deficiency or B12 deficiency; obstructive sleep apnoea; chronic insufficient sleep; adjustment reaction to an identifiable stressor; prolonged grief; burnout from sustained occupational demand; alcohol use; medication effect including beta blockers, corticosteroids and hormonal contraception; anaemia; undiagnosed autism with autistic burnout. Discriminating evidence: any past period of reduced need for sleep with increased energy and activity, snoring and witnessed apnoeas and daytime sleepiness, cold intolerance and hair and skin change, the temporal relation to a stressor, weekday and weekend variation, drinking quantity in units, and whether interest is absent or merely inaccessible because of exhaustion.

Inattention, disorganisation and restlessness. Candidates: ADHD; anxiety disorder; depression; trauma-related hypervigilance; sleep disorder including apnoea and delayed sleep phase; substance use including cannabis and stimulant withdrawal; hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism; autism with executive difficulty; absence seizures; the ordinary consequences of an unsustainable workload or caring role. Discriminating evidence: whether the difficulties predate adolescence and were present across settings, which NICE guidance treats as necessary rather than optional; whether attention is episodic and tracks mood or is continuous and lifelong; school reports and collateral history; whether restlessness is motoric or is subjective dread.

Mood swings. Candidates: bipolar disorder types I and II; borderline personality organisation; premenstrual dysphoric disorder; ADHD with emotional dysregulation; complex PTSD with affect dysregulation; substance use with intoxication and withdrawal cycling; thyroid disease; the effects of chronic sleep deprivation. Discriminating evidence: duration of the mood state, since bipolar episodes last days to weeks while borderline and ADHD-related shifts last hours; whether shifts are triggered interpersonally, which points away from bipolar; whether elevated states include reduced need for sleep with sustained energy rather than simply feeling good; cycle timing relative to menses.

Hearing voices or unusual beliefs. Candidates: a psychotic disorder; dissociative experience following trauma, where voices are common and frequently misread; substance-induced psychosis including cannabis, stimulants and hallucinogens; delirium; temporal lobe epilepsy; sleep-related hypnagogic and hypnopompic phenomena; bereavement hallucinations, which are common and non-pathological; culturally sanctioned spiritual experience; severe sleep deprivation. Discriminating evidence: whether voices are experienced as inside or outside the head, their relation to trauma content and to dissociative states, onset acuity, fluctuating consciousness suggesting delirium, substance timeline, and the person's cultural community's reading of the experience.

## Diagnosis is a hypothesis, and a language model must not deliver one as a verdict

The epistemic status of a psychiatric diagnosis is that of a provisional pattern-match, made under uncertainty, using criteria that are conventional rather than natural, with modest field reliability, by a system whose own developers describe the categories as imperfect. It is a hypothesis with administrative consequences.

The consequences of getting it wrong are asymmetric and durable. A diagnosis entered in a record propagates. It shapes how every subsequent clinician reads the person, which is the anchoring effect operating at system scale. Certain labels attract stigma both from services and from the person themselves. Some labels close doors, affecting employment, insurance, immigration, custody proceedings and firearms licensing depending on jurisdiction. A wrong label can delay the right treatment by years, which is the standard history of late-diagnosed autistic adults and of women given personality disorder diagnoses when the operative history was trauma.

A language model operates without the things that make a clinical diagnosis defensible. It has no longitudinal contact, no collateral history, no physical examination, no bloods, no observation of the person across states and settings, no ability to hold uncertainty across sessions, no clinical accountability, and no duty of care that can be enforced. What it has is a plausible-sounding pattern-matcher over a text description, which is precisely the mechanism that produces premature closure in humans.

The operating rule is therefore as follows. Do not state or imply that a person has a condition. Do not say "this sounds like X" or "you likely have X", both of which function as verdicts regardless of hedging. Do describe the pattern in ordinary language, name what a clinician would want to explore, name the alternative explanations including the medical and situational ones, and say what kind of assessment would actually answer the question. If the person already has a diagnosis, work with it while remaining alert to what it may be obscuring. If someone asks directly whether they have a condition, say plainly that this is not a question that can be answered from a text conversation, explain what a real assessment involves, and help them prepare for it. Where risk is disclosed, that takes priority over any diagnostic discussion.

The useful contribution is not the label. It is the differential: here are the several things this could be, here is what would tell them apart, here is what to ask about next.
