## Why context changes the formulation rather than decorating it

The same set of behaviours means different things in different contexts, and the context is not background detail to be noted after the formulation is written. It determines what the formulation is.

Hypervigilance in a person who is safe is a symptom. Hypervigilance in a person who is currently being stalked is accurate threat detection. Social withdrawal in a person with a rich prior social life is a change requiring explanation. Social withdrawal in an autistic adult recovering capacity after sustained overload is a functional strategy. Emotional volatility in a person with stable circumstances is a regulation problem. Emotional volatility in someone facing eviction while caring for a dying parent is a proportionate response to load. Suspiciousness of services in a person whose community has been repeatedly harmed by those services is not paranoia.

Four questions convert context from decoration into formulation content. What is the person currently responding to, in their actual environment, right now? What did they learn to expect from other people, and when did they learn it? What is normative for their developmental stage, their culture and their circumstances? What material conditions are constraining what they can do?

A formulation that answers none of these has located all the causal action inside the individual, which is both usually wrong and specifically harmful. It converts a social problem into a personal defect, which is the mechanism Johnstone and Boyle (2018) identify as the core failure of individualised medical framing. The corrective is not to abandon individual psychology but to establish what proportion of the variance actually sits in the environment before assigning the rest to the person.

The sections that follow cover the contextual domains that most often change a formulation: attachment and developmental history, life stage, trauma, neurodivergence, culture and migration, structural and material conditions, family systems, and the medical and pharmacological causes that produce psychiatric presentations.

## Attachment: what the models license and what they do not

Attachment theory, originating with Bowlby and operationalised by Ainsworth, holds that infants form organised strategies for obtaining care from a caregiver under conditions of distress, and that these strategies reflect what has worked with that caregiver. Ainsworth's Strange Situation procedure, a structured sequence of separations and reunions, produced three organised classifications: secure, avoidant, and anxious or ambivalent (Ainsworth et al., 1978). Main and Solomon (1990) added a fourth, disorganised or disoriented, for infants whose behaviour showed contradiction, freezing, or apprehension toward the caregiver and did not fit the organised categories. Main and Solomon were explicit that the disorganised classification was not of the same kind as the others and advised that an underlying organised classification be specified where possible.

Main and Hesse proposed that disorganisation arises when a distressed child both wants to approach the caregiver for comfort and remembers occasions when the caregiver frightened them, producing an unresolvable conflict between approach and avoidance. Main, George and Kaplan developed the Adult Attachment Interview to assess adult states of mind regarding attachment, coded not on the content of the childhood account but on its coherence.

The clinically useful content of this literature is about strategies rather than types. Ask what the person learned to do with distress. Someone whose distress was consistently met learned that expressing it works and that others can be approached. Someone whose distress was met with rejection or intrusion learned to minimise and manage it alone, which presents in adulthood as difficulty identifying feelings, discomfort with dependence, and a preference for self-reliance that persists past the point of usefulness. Someone whose care was unpredictable learned to escalate to obtain a response, which presents as heightened distress expression, difficulty being soothed, and vigilance about the availability of others. Someone whose source of comfort was also a source of fear has no coherent strategy, which presents as approach and withdrawal in the same relationship, and as difficulty in exactly the relationships that matter most, including the therapeutic one.

The limits require stating clearly. Infant classifications do not map cleanly onto adult categories, and the popular four-box adult attachment style taxonomy circulating outside the research literature is not the same construct as the research instruments and should not be treated as though it were. Attachment classification is not a diagnosis and not a fixed trait. It is relationship-specific rather than person-general, so a person may have different strategies with different people. Classifications change with circumstances. The disorganised category in particular has been over-extended in popular use well beyond what the coding system supports, and it should not be inferred from an adult's self-description.

The legitimate use in formulation is to explain why a particular relational configuration is threatening, why help is hard to accept, why the therapeutic relationship follows a predictable shape, and why some interventions that require tolerating closeness will fail before that is addressed. The illegitimate use is to assign someone a type and then explain everything by it.

## Developmental stage and life-stage transitions

What counts as a problem depends on what is normative for the person's stage, and the same behaviour can be developmentally expected at one age and a signal at another.

In early and middle childhood, separation distress, specific fears, magical thinking, imaginary companions and concrete reasoning about causation are normative. What warrants attention is loss of previously acquired skills, developmental regression, and functional impairment across settings rather than in one.

In adolescence, increased risk-taking, sleep phase delay, intense affect, identity experimentation, conflict with caregivers and peer orientation are normative rather than pathological. Adolescent sleep phase delay is a biological shift, not laziness, and school timetables that ignore it generate presentations that resemble depression. Identity questioning, including about gender and sexuality, is developmental work. The genuine signals in adolescence are self-harm, marked functional decline, social withdrawal from all peers rather than a change of peer group, and psychotic-like experiences that persist outside of substance use or sleep deprivation.

Emerging adulthood carries its own predictable crises around autonomy, occupational identity, and separation from family, and these are frequently misread as disorder when they are transitional.

Adulthood transitions that reliably produce presentations include becoming a parent, which involves sleep deprivation, identity reorganisation, relationship restructuring and, for the birthing parent, substantial hormonal change; the perimenopausal transition, which produces mood, sleep, cognitive and anxiety symptoms that are frequently attributed to primary psychiatric illness; caregiving for an ageing parent; redundancy and career disruption; the departure of children; and retirement, which removes structure, identity and social contact simultaneously.

In later life, the discriminating questions change. Cognitive complaints require differentiation between depression, delirium and neurocognitive disorder, with course being the primary discriminator: acute and fluctuating suggests delirium, subacute with prominent low mood and preserved effort suggests depression, and gradual and progressive suggests neurocognitive disorder. Grief and cumulative bereavement become common precipitants. Sensory impairment produces social withdrawal and can produce hallucinations that are not psychotic. Polypharmacy becomes a leading cause of psychiatric presentation.

Erikson's psychosocial stage model provides a rough map of the task at each period, and it retains value as a reminder that adult development continues rather than as an empirically precise account. Treat stage models as prompts to ask what the person's current developmental task is, not as a schedule they should be meeting.

## Trauma and adverse childhood experiences

The Adverse Childhood Experiences study surveyed adults enrolled in a health maintenance organisation about seven categories of childhood adversity: psychological, physical and sexual abuse, violence against the mother, and living with household members who were substance users, mentally ill or suicidal, or imprisoned (Felitti et al., 1998). The finding that reoriented the field was a graded dose-response relationship between the number of adversity categories and a wide range of adult health and social outcomes, including several leading causes of death. Adversity was common in a middle-class insured population, which contradicted the assumption that it was concentrated in specific groups.

Two implications matter for formulation. First, the relationship is dose-dependent and cumulative rather than specific, so the count of adversity types carries more prognostic weight than the specific type. Second, the outcomes are not confined to mental health; they include physical disease, health behaviours and social outcomes, which is why trauma histories belong in formulations of presentations that appear entirely physical.

The ACE framework has real limits that should be stated. The original categories omit community violence, poverty, racism, bullying, medical trauma, migration and displacement, and neglect is under-represented relative to abuse. A count treats all adversities as equivalent, which they are not. The measure is retrospective and self-reported. ACE scores are epidemiologically informative and clinically weak as an individual predictor; a high score does not tell you what a specific person needs, and a zero score does not exclude trauma. Recent work has examined which combinations of adversities carry particular risk rather than treating the total as the unit.

The clinically important extension is that trauma is not confined to discrete events. Prolonged, repeated, interpersonal trauma from which escape was not possible produces a picture that single-incident PTSD criteria do not capture. ICD-11 addresses this with complex PTSD, requiring the core PTSD features plus disturbances in self-organisation: affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and disturbances in relationships (Reed et al., 2019). DSM-5-TR has no equivalent category, which means that in DSM-based systems this presentation is routinely coded as a personality disorder instead.

That coding difference has consequences. The same person may receive a complex PTSD formulation that locates the difficulty in what was done to them, or a borderline personality disorder diagnosis that locates it in who they are, depending on which manual the clinician uses. Both may describe the same phenomena. The framing determines the treatment offered, the attitude of services, and the person's understanding of themselves.

## Trauma-informed and trauma-focused are different things

These terms are frequently used interchangeably and mean different things, and the distinction is operationally important.

Trauma-informed refers to how a service or a clinician operates, not to what they treat. SAMHSA's framework defines a trauma-informed approach as one that realises the widespread impact of trauma, recognises its signs, responds by integrating that knowledge into practice, and actively resists re-traumatisation (SAMHSA, 2014). It sets out six principles: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; and attention to cultural, historical and gender issues.

In practice, being trauma-informed means assuming that trauma may be present without requiring disclosure, providing predictability and control over the pace and content of the conversation, explaining what will happen before it happens, avoiding procedures that replicate powerlessness, and asking what happened to a person rather than what is wrong with them. It applies to everyone regardless of known history, because the history is frequently unknown.

Trauma-focused refers to a class of interventions that directly process traumatic memory. Trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR and narrative exposure therapy are trauma-focused. These treatments have substantial evidence for PTSD and they involve deliberately approaching the traumatic material, which is distressing by design.

The distinction matters because the two have different indications. Everyone should be worked with in a trauma-informed way. Not everyone should receive trauma-focused treatment, and not everyone who should receive it should receive it now. Trauma-focused work presupposes sufficient stability, safety and regulation capacity to tolerate the processing. Delivering it to someone in ongoing danger, in acute crisis, in unstable housing, or without the capacity to return to baseline afterwards can cause harm.

The common errors run in both directions. One is treating trauma-informed as a synonym for gentle, so that the service becomes cautious and never addresses the trauma at all, which leaves people with untreated PTSD for years. The other is moving to trauma processing before stabilisation, or requiring a person to give a detailed trauma account during an initial assessment when there is no capacity to contain what that produces. A person should never have to disclose the content of their trauma in order to be treated as though trauma might be relevant.

## Neurodivergence: autism, and how it is misread

Autism is a developmental difference in social communication, sensory processing, cognitive style and need for predictability, present from early development, though it may not become impairing until environmental demands exceed the person's compensatory capacity. That last clause explains most late diagnosis in adults.

Camouflaging, also called masking, is the conscious and unconscious suppression of autistic behaviour and the substitution of learned non-autistic behaviour in order to appear typical. It is more frequently observed in autistic girls and women and is a documented contributor to missed, late and incorrect diagnosis (Hull et al.; reviews of late and missed diagnosis in autistic girls and women). Camouflaging is effortful and its costs are cumulative, producing exhaustion, loss of identity, and the state commonly described as autistic burnout, which presents as sudden loss of previously available capacity and is routinely diagnosed as depression.

The presentations most often misread. Autistic burnout is read as depression, because the surface features overlap; the discriminator is that burnout follows a period of sustained excessive demand, involves loss of skills rather than loss of pleasure, and improves with reduction of demand rather than with behavioural activation. Sensory distress and overload are read as anxiety disorder; the discriminator is that the trigger is sensory rather than appraisal-based and the response is immediate rather than anticipatory. Distress at change and need for routine are read as obsessive-compulsive disorder; the discriminator is that the behaviour is not experienced as unwanted or intrusive and serves predictability rather than neutralising a feared outcome. Difficulty with reciprocal social interaction is read as social anxiety; the discriminator is whether the difficulty is fear of negative evaluation or absence of intuitive access to the social rules. Meltdowns and shutdowns are read as emotional dysregulation of a borderline type. Restricted eating driven by sensory aversion is read as an eating disorder driven by body image.

The most consequential misreading is autism as borderline personality disorder in women. A conceptual analysis of late, missed and misdiagnosis in autistic girls and women identifies borderline personality disorder as a specific factor in this pathway (PMC11861065). The two can also genuinely co-occur, and there is a plausible developmental route by which sustained social invalidation of an unrecognised autistic person produces a personality presentation that meets borderline criteria. Recent comparative work has examined camouflaging and pragmatic language differences between the two groups as potential discriminators (Gracia et al., 2026).

The discriminating questions are developmental and functional. Were the social difficulties present from early childhood and across all settings? Is the social difficulty effortful compensation that has always been required, or a loss of a capacity that was previously easy? Is the emotional instability triggered by sensory and demand load, or by interpersonal rejection cues? Does the person describe sensory experience that others do not share? Has the person been exhausted by social contact for as long as they can remember?

## Neurodivergence: ADHD, and how it is misread

ADHD is a developmental condition involving difficulties with attentional regulation, inhibition, working memory, activation and, in many people, emotional regulation. NICE guidance requires that adult ADHD be diagnosed only when symptoms are not better explained by another condition, and that assessment establish that difficulties predate adolescence and are present across settings (NICE NG87).

The presentations most often misread run in both directions. ADHD read as something else: chronic underperformance and self-criticism read as depression; restlessness and racing thoughts read as anxiety; emotional dysregulation and rejection sensitivity read as borderline personality disorder; short mood shifts read as bipolar disorder; disorganisation and lateness read as a character problem. Something else read as ADHD: attentional difficulty secondary to depression, anxiety, trauma, sleep disorder, substance use or thyroid dysfunction.

The discriminators are chronology and pervasiveness. ADHD symptoms are lifelong, continuous and present across settings. Attentional difficulty secondary to mood or anxiety is episodic and tracks the mood or anxiety, and it did not predate it. Bipolar mood elevation is episodic over days to weeks and includes reduced need for sleep with sustained energy; ADHD-related mood shifts last minutes to hours and are usually reactive to events. Trauma-related concentration difficulty is typically accompanied by hypervigilance, intrusions and a datable onset.

Collateral information is decisive here in a way that self-report is not: school reports, the account of someone who knew the person in childhood, and documented early functioning discriminate better than any adult questionnaire.

Co-occurrence is the norm rather than the exception. Autism and ADHD co-occur frequently. Both co-occur with anxiety, depression, substance use and trauma sequelae. Deciding that one explanation is present does not license dropping the others.

The reason accurate identification matters is that the intervention logic differs. If executive difficulty is the primary problem, treating the resulting depression without addressing the executive difficulty leaves the generating mechanism running. If the person has spent decades attributing their difficulty to laziness or moral failure, accurate identification changes the self-concept work substantially. Accommodations, medication where indicated, and environmental restructuring are available only after identification.

## Culture, cultural concepts of distress, and the cultural formulation

Culture shapes which experiences are considered distressing, how distress is expressed, what explanations are available for it, what help is sought, and what recovery looks like. A clinician working outside their own cultural frame who applies their own norms will systematically misread both the presence and the absence of pathology.

DSM-5 addresses this through the Outline for Cultural Formulation and the Cultural Formulation Interview. The Outline covers cultural identity, cultural concepts of distress, cultural stressors and supports, cultural features of the clinician-patient relationship, and overall assessment. The core Cultural Formulation Interview is a sixteen-question protocol with clinician instructions, accompanied by an informant version and twelve supplementary modules covering topics including specific life-cycle populations (American Psychiatric Association; Lewis-Fernández et al.).

The interview's underlying logic is to elicit the person's own framing before applying a clinical one. It asks what brings them, what they call the problem, what they believe causes it, what others in their community think, what makes it better or worse, what help they have sought, what has helped, what has got in the way, and what they want.

Practical points that change formulations. Somatic presentation of psychological distress is normative in many cultures and is not a failure of psychological insight or an indication of alexithymia. Explanatory models involving spiritual, religious, moral or interpersonal causation are common and should be worked with rather than corrected; the clinician does not need to share the model to use it. Experiences that would be treated as psychotic in one context, including hearing the voice of a deceased relative or receiving communication in a religious frame, may be sanctioned and unremarkable in another, and the discriminator is the reaction of the person's own community rather than the clinician's judgement. Family-centred rather than individual-centred decision-making is normative in many cultures and is not enmeshment. Help-seeking thresholds and the sequence in which formal services are approached vary widely.

The counter-error is cultural attribution, in which genuine pathology or genuine harm is dismissed as cultural. Domestic violence, coercive control, child abuse and untreated psychosis do not become acceptable because they are common in a community. The test is whether the person themselves, and the people in their community who are most affected, regard the situation as harmful.

## Migration, acculturation and immigration status

Migration produces a distinctive set of stressors that do not reduce to any single diagnostic category: loss of social role and occupational status, separation from family, language barriers, discrimination in the receiving country, insecure legal status, and in the case of forced migration, pre-migration trauma and often traumatic transit.

Berry's model describes four acculturation strategies, defined by whether the person maintains their culture of origin and whether they engage with the receiving culture. Integration maintains both. Assimilation adopts the new and relinquishes the original. Separation retains the original and rejects the new. Marginalisation involves neither. Systematic review evidence associates marginalisation with the worst mental health outcomes and integration with the best, with marginalisation substantially raising the likelihood of anxiety-related symptoms relative to integration, and separation also raising it (systematic review of acculturation strategies and migrant mental health, 2020).

The critical qualification is that acculturation strategy is not simply a choice. Whether integration is available depends on whether the receiving society permits it. A person facing housing discrimination, employment exclusion and racial harassment has not chosen separation. Formulating an acculturation strategy as an individual coping style, without accounting for the structural conditions that constrain it, reproduces the error of locating social causes inside the person. The same review identifies low education, host-language proficiency and financial hardship as contributors to acculturative stress.

Immigration status itself is a formulation-relevant variable with direct psychological effects. Insecure or temporary status produces chronic uncertainty, restricts access to healthcare, employment and benefits, and in many jurisdictions creates a rational fear of engaging with statutory services including mental health services. Detention, dispersal and the asylum determination process are themselves adverse experiences. A person with precarious status who avoids services and minimises symptoms is behaving rationally.

For formulation, ask directly about status where it may be relevant, about what services the person can actually access, about family separation, and about whether the person's fear of institutions is realistic. Understand that intolerance of uncertainty in someone awaiting an asylum decision is not a cognitive distortion.

## Structural and material conditions

Poverty, housing insecurity, unemployment, insecure work, debt, discrimination and neighbourhood deprivation are causally related to mental health outcomes and not merely correlated with them. Compton and Shim's account of the social determinants of mental health covers discrimination and social exclusion, adverse early life experiences, poor education, unemployment and job insecurity, income inequality, poverty, neighbourhood deprivation, food insecurity, poor housing quality and instability, adverse features of the built environment, and poor access to care.

Several of these operate through mechanisms that are directly relevant to formulation. Chronic financial scarcity consumes cognitive capacity, which degrades exactly the executive functioning that most psychological interventions require the person to deploy. Housing insecurity removes the safety that stabilisation work presupposes. Insecure and unpredictable work removes routine and control, both of which are protective. Food insecurity affects sleep, concentration and mood directly. Discrimination operates both as discrete stressful events and as chronic vigilance, and structural or institutional racism operates through differential access to goods, services and opportunities embedded in policy and practice rather than through individual prejudice.

Two formulation errors are common. The first is omission, in which the material conditions are recorded in the social history and then play no part in the explanatory account. The second is fatalism, in which the structural framing is used to conclude that nothing can be done psychologically, when in fact both structural intervention and psychological intervention have roles.

The practical stance. Establish the material facts before formulating: income and its security, housing and its security, food, debt, work conditions, caring responsibilities, immigration status, experience of discrimination. Where a material condition is doing the causal work, name it as such in the formulation. Prioritise practical intervention, including welfare advice, housing advocacy and debt support, where those will do more than therapy. Do not offer psychological techniques for problems whose solution is material, and do not treat the absence of improvement as a failure of the person when their circumstances have not changed. Where structural conditions cannot be changed, psychological work on the impact of those conditions is legitimate, but it should be named honestly as that rather than framed as correcting distorted thinking about a situation the person is reading accurately.

## Family and relational systems

Systemic thinking shifts the unit of analysis from the individual to the relationships around them. The core move is from linear causation, in which A causes B, to circular causation, in which patterns are maintained by mutually reinforcing loops with no single origin. A parent's monitoring and a young person's concealment escalate each other, and asking which came first is less useful than describing the loop.

Several systemic concepts carry directly into formulation. The identified patient is the family member who carries the symptom on behalf of a system under strain, so that the person referred is not necessarily where the difficulty is located. Boundaries describe how permeable the divisions are between family subsystems, with both excessive rigidity and excessive diffusion generating difficulty; structural family therapy, associated with Salvador Minuchin, is built on this analysis. Bowen family systems theory contributes differentiation of self, the capacity to maintain a separate position while remaining emotionally connected, and triangulation, in which tension between two people is stabilised by drawing in a third. [The primary sources for Minuchin and Bowen were not retrieved in this pass; treat the attributions as standard but the specific formulations as UNVERIFIED.]

Intergenerational transmission is the observation that relational patterns, roles and unresolved losses recur across generations, and the genogram is the standard tool for mapping this: three generations, with relationships, deaths, separations, illnesses, migrations and family narratives recorded alongside the structure.

Practical uses in individual formulation. Ask who else is involved in the problem and what they do when it happens. Ask what would change for other people if the person got better, because a system organised around a person's difficulty can inadvertently maintain it. Ask what role the person occupies in the family, since roles such as the responsible one, the difficult one and the fragile one are assigned early and persist. Ask what is not discussed in the family, because the content of silence is frequently the content of the difficulty.

Two cautions. Systemic formulation must not become family blame, and in particular it must not be used to reframe abuse as a mutually maintained pattern; where there is a power differential or violence, circular framing is inappropriate and harmful. Systemic accounts also risk under-weighting individual factors including neurodevelopmental difference and physical illness, both of which shape family patterns rather than resulting from them.

## Medical and organic conditions that present as psychiatric illness

A psychiatric presentation should not be accepted as psychiatric until medical causes have been considered, and this is the most consequential single omission in formulation. Standard guidance on medical assessment of patients with psychiatric symptoms directs a full physical and neurological examination plus baseline investigation including blood count, glucose, thyroid function, liver function, calcium and vitamin B12, with thyroid testing specifically indicated for new-onset psychiatric symptoms in patients over forty (Merck Manual Professional Edition).

Thyroid dysfunction is the most common mimic. Hypothyroidism produces fatigue, low mood, cognitive slowing, weight gain and cold intolerance, and is routinely diagnosed as depression. Hyperthyroidism produces anxiety, agitation, insomnia, tremor, weight loss and palpitations, and is routinely diagnosed as an anxiety disorder.

Vitamin B12 deficiency produces a wide range of neuropsychiatric presentations including irritability, confusion, impaired concentration, insomnia, amnesia, delirium, dementia, psychosis and mood disorder, and it can do so without anaemia being present, which means a normal haemoglobin does not exclude it (case literature on B12 deficiency presenting as psychosis and as dementia).

Obstructive sleep apnoea produces daytime sleepiness, fatigue, low energy, concentration difficulty, irritability and low mood, and overlaps so heavily with depression that it is both frequently missed in psychiatric populations and frequently mislabelled. The discriminating features are snoring, witnessed apnoeas, morning headache, and unrefreshing sleep of adequate duration.

Delirium is the presentation that must never be missed. Acute or subacute onset, fluctuating level of consciousness and attention, disturbed sleep-wake cycle, and perceptual disturbance indicate an underlying medical cause, commonly infection, metabolic disturbance, medication or withdrawal, and it is a medical emergency rather than a psychiatric one.

Other conditions with well-documented psychiatric presentations include anaemia and iron deficiency, diabetes and hypoglycaemia, adrenal disorders, autoimmune and inflammatory conditions, epilepsy and particularly temporal lobe epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, HIV, syphilis, hepatic and renal impairment, sleep disorders other than apnoea, chronic pain, and the perimenopausal and postpartum hormonal transitions.

The features that should raise medical suspicion sharply: first onset of psychiatric symptoms after the age of forty, particularly of psychosis; abrupt onset without psychosocial precipitant; fluctuating consciousness or attention; any focal neurological sign; visual, olfactory or tactile hallucinations rather than auditory; unexplained weight change; abnormal vital signs; failure to respond to adequate psychiatric treatment; and a presentation that does not fit any recognised psychiatric pattern.

## Substances, withdrawal, and medication effects

Substance use and its withdrawal states produce every major psychiatric presentation, and they are systematically under-asked about and under-reported.

Alcohol produces depression during use and anxiety, agitation, insomnia and tremor during withdrawal; heavy withdrawal can produce seizures and delirium tremens and is medically dangerous. Cannabis produces anxiety, paranoia and, in vulnerable individuals, psychosis, with withdrawal producing irritability, insomnia and appetite change. Stimulants produce anxiety, insomnia, paranoia and psychosis during use, and profound depression, hypersomnia and anhedonia during withdrawal that closely resembles a depressive episode. Opioid withdrawal produces intense dysphoria, anxiety and agitation. Benzodiazepine withdrawal produces severe anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbance and seizure risk, and is protracted. High caffeine intake produces anxiety and insomnia and is almost never asked about.

The reasoning rule is temporal. Establish which came first, whether the psychiatric symptoms persist during sustained abstinence, and whether the symptom timeline tracks the use timeline. Symptoms that resolve within weeks of abstinence were substance-related; symptoms that persist beyond that are more likely primary. Self-medication and primary disorder are frequently both present, and treating either alone tends to fail.

Prescribed medications are the second under-considered contributor. Corticosteroids produce mood elevation, irritability, depression and psychosis. Beta blockers produce fatigue and low mood. Hormonal contraceptives and hormone therapy affect mood in a subset of users. Isotretinoin has documented psychiatric associations. Interferon produces depression. Anticholinergic burden produces cognitive impairment and, in older adults, delirium. Antipsychotics produce akathisia, which is an intensely distressing inner restlessness routinely mistaken for anxiety or agitation and treated by increasing the dose that caused it, and they produce emotional blunting and sedation that are recorded as negative symptoms. Antidepressants can produce activation, emotional blunting, sexual dysfunction and, in people with undiagnosed bipolarity, switching into elevated mood. Discontinuation and withdrawal effects from antidepressants are frequently misread as relapse.

The practical questions: what are they taking, prescribed and otherwise, at what dose, since when, what changed recently, and does the symptom timeline align with any of those changes.

## A contextual checklist for any formulation

Run these before finalising an account. Each has changed a formulation often enough to justify the time.

Development and relationships. What was the person's early caregiving environment, and what did they learn to do with distress? What is their developmental stage and what is its task? What transitions are currently underway?

Trauma. Has anything happened to this person that would explain this? Was it single-incident or prolonged and interpersonal? Is it over, or ongoing? Are you working in a trauma-informed way regardless of whether trauma is known?

Neurodivergence. Were these difficulties lifelong and present across settings? Is there sensory difference, effortful social compensation, or executive difficulty predating everything else? Has the person been given a personality or mood diagnosis that a developmental explanation would fit better?

Culture. What does the person call this problem and what do they think caused it? What does their community think? Are you reading a normative practice as pathology, or dismissing genuine harm as cultural?

Migration and status. Is immigration status insecure? What can they actually access? Is separation from family part of the picture?

Structure and material conditions. Income, housing, food, debt, work security, caring load, discrimination. Is a material condition doing the causal work, and if so is it named in the formulation?

Family and system. Who else is in this pattern and what do they do when it happens? What would change for others if the person recovered? Is there a power differential or violence that makes circular framing inappropriate?

Medical and pharmacological. Has anyone examined them and taken bloods? Thyroid, B12, full blood count, glucose. Do they snore? What is the alcohol intake in units? What substances, at what frequency? What medications, and what changed recently? Is there anything suggesting delirium or a neurological cause?

If a formulation cannot answer most of these, it is provisional in a stronger sense than usual, and the missing information should be stated as part of the account rather than left implicit.
