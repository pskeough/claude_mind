## What a formulation is and what it does that a diagnosis cannot

A formulation is a hypothesis about a person's difficulties that links psychological theory to that specific person's history and circumstances, and that guides what to do next. The British Psychological Society's Division of Clinical Psychology defines it in exactly these terms and treats it as a core competence rather than an optional extra (Division of Clinical Psychology, 2011). The defining feature is explanatory. A diagnosis says which category a set of complaints falls into. A formulation says why this person, with this history, has arrived at these difficulties now, and what is keeping them going.

Macneil, Hasty, Conus and Berk (2012) set out the clinical argument for this in BMC Medicine. Two people with the same diagnosis can require entirely different interventions, because the causal and maintaining processes differ. Diagnosis is a poor guide to what to do; formulation is built to be a guide to what to do. Their position is not that diagnosis is worthless but that it is insufficient on its own.

A usable formulation has several properties. It is provisional, meaning it is stated as a best current account and expected to change. It is parsimonious, in that it explains the presenting difficulties without importing every fact about the person's life. It is testable, in that it makes predictions about what should change if a particular process is targeted. It is shared, in that the person can recognise themselves in it and can say where it is wrong. It carries an intervention implication, so that reading the formulation tells you what the first treatment target should be.

The empirical status of formulation is weaker than its professional status. Inter-rater reliability studies of formulation have produced mixed results, and reviews have noted that poor reliability is partly attributable to the absence of an agreed definition of what a formulation must contain (Division of Clinical Psychology, 2011). A consensus study of clinical psychologists found continuing disagreement about which components matter, patterned by theoretical orientation and years of experience (Thrower et al., 2024). Two competent clinicians formulating the same case will often produce different accounts. This is a real limitation and not a reason to abandon formulation, but it does mean that a formulation should be presented as one plausible account rather than as the correct reading of a person.

For a language model, the practical consequence is that a formulation is the appropriate output when someone describes a difficulty and wants understanding, whereas a diagnosis is not. A formulation can be offered tentatively, checked against the person's own sense of themselves, and revised. It does not carry the institutional weight or the risk of a diagnostic label delivered by a system with no clinical accountability.

## The 5 Ps: presenting, predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, protective

The 5 Ps framework organises a case along a time axis and asks five questions of any presentation. Its lineage runs through Weerasekera's multiperspective grid, which set out four Ps arranged against biological, psychological and social perspectives (Weerasekera, 1993, 1996), and it is most often cited in its five-element form via Macneil, Hasty, Conus and Berk (2012).

The presenting problem is what the person is actually struggling with, stated in their own terms and at the level of concrete difficulty rather than diagnosis. "I cannot make myself leave the house on work days and I have used all my leave" is a presenting problem. "Agoraphobia" is not.

Predisposing factors are the longstanding vulnerabilities that made this presentation more likely. They include genetic and constitutional loading, early attachment experiences, chronic adversity, neurodevelopmental difference, health conditions, and the beliefs about self and others laid down in childhood. Predisposing factors explain why this person rather than another.

Precipitating factors are the proximal triggers. They are what changed in the weeks or months before things got worse. Job loss, bereavement, a relationship ending, a medication change, a relapse into substance use, a move, an anniversary, an assault. Precipitants explain why now rather than a year ago.

Perpetuating factors are what keeps the problem going once it has started, and they are the most clinically important of the five because they are usually the most modifiable. Avoidance, safety behaviours, rumination, sleep disruption, withdrawal from reinforcing activity, substance use that suppresses distress in the short term, relational patterns that inadvertently reward the problem behaviour, and the material consequences of the difficulty itself such as debt or job loss.

Protective factors are the person's resources: skills, relationships, values, prior recovery, employment, stable housing, physical health, a sense of purpose. They matter for prognosis, for risk assessment, and for choosing an intervention the person can actually sustain.

The 5 Ps is the right tool when you need a fast, theory-neutral map that anyone in a multidisciplinary team can read, when you are formulating a complex presentation early with incomplete information, or when you need a structure that holds biological, psychological and social material together without committing to one model. Its limits are real. It is descriptive rather than explanatory. It produces a list of factors and does not specify the mechanism linking them, so it can degenerate into a filing exercise where information is sorted into boxes with no account of how anything causes anything else. It has no built-in theory to say which perpetuating factor to target first. Treat it as scaffolding for a more specific model rather than as a finished formulation.

## Worked mini-example: 5 Ps

A 34 year old software developer presents saying he has been signed off work for six weeks with what his doctor called burnout, that he cannot concentrate, sleeps badly, has stopped seeing friends, and is drinking most evenings. He says he feels like a fraud and expects to be dismissed.

Presenting problem. Six weeks off work, unable to concentrate or complete tasks, initial insomnia and early waking, social withdrawal, six to eight units of alcohol most evenings, intense anticipatory dread about returning, persistent thoughts that he is not competent and will be found out.

Predisposing. Father was highly critical and set performance conditions on approval; the client describes never having been praised for effort, only for results. He was streamed into accelerated classes and identified as "the clever one", so competence became the basis of his worth. He has a long history of working late and checking work repeatedly. He describes himself as having always been anxious about evaluation. No prior treatment episodes and no family psychiatric history reported.

Precipitating. A reorganisation eight months ago put him under a new manager who gave critical feedback in a team meeting. Three months ago a project he led shipped with a defect that caused a customer outage. He was not blamed but has replayed the incident continuously since.

Perpetuating. He checks his code repeatedly and reworks completed tasks, which lengthens his hours and lowers his output, which confirms the belief that he is failing. He has stopped attending team meetings where he might be asked to account for progress, so he never obtains evidence that his standing is intact. Evening alcohol reduces rumination for two hours and worsens sleep, which degrades next-day concentration. Social withdrawal has removed the friendships that used to contradict the fraud belief. Being off work removes any disconfirming information about his competence and increases the anticipated cost of return.

Protective. He has a stable ten-year relationship and a partner who is supportive and involved. He has fifteen years of documented good performance and two promotions. He has no debt and secure housing. He engaged readily with assessment and could describe his thinking with unusual clarity. He has a history of endurance running that he enjoyed and stopped four months ago.

The formulation reads: longstanding conditional worth contingent on performance, activated by critical feedback and a public failure, now maintained by a checking and avoidance cycle that prevents disconfirmation, compounded by alcohol-disrupted sleep and loss of reinforcing activity. First targets are the sleep and alcohol cycle, graded re-exposure to work contact, and reinstating the running. The core belief work about conditional worth comes later.

## The integrative formulation: Johnstone and Dallos

Johnstone and Dallos (2014) treat formulation not as a technique belonging to one model but as a process of making sense of a person's problems that can and often should draw on more than one theoretical framework. Their edited volume covers cognitive behavioural, psychodynamic, systemic, narrative and social constructionist formulation, and devotes two chapters to integration, including a dynamic multi-level approach by Dallos, Stedmon and Johnstone.

The core commitments of their position are as follows. Formulation is a process rather than a product, developed over time in the relationship rather than written once after assessment. It is collaborative, meaning the person's own account of their difficulties is data rather than material to be corrected. It is reflective, meaning the clinician's own reactions and position are part of what is being examined. It attends to relational and social context, so that a formulation which locates all the causal action inside the individual is treated as incomplete. It centres personal meaning, so that the question is not only what happened but what the person made of what happened.

Integration in their sense is not eclectic mixing. The claim is that different models operate at different levels of description and that a good integrative formulation specifies which level each element belongs to. A behavioural account of a safety behaviour, an attachment account of why closeness is threatening, and a systemic account of how the family currently organises around the problem can all be true at once, because they are answers to different questions. What makes integration coherent rather than muddled is being explicit about which question each part of the formulation is answering.

The related idea of team formulation runs through this literature and through the BPS guidance. Team formulation is the practice of a whole staff group building a shared psychological account of a person, often someone whose presentation has provoked strong and divergent staff reactions. Its value is less in the document produced than in shifting a team from a description of a person as difficult toward an account of what the person is responding to.

This is the right approach for complex, long-standing, multi-agency presentations where no single model accounts for the picture, and in contexts where the person has already had multiple diagnoses that have not helped. Its limits are that it is time-expensive, that it demands genuine competence in several models rather than surface familiarity with each, and that its openness makes it harder to evaluate. An integrative formulation is difficult to test because it can absorb almost any new information without being disconfirmed. Guard against that by stating in advance what would count as evidence against the account.

## The cognitive behavioural formulation: Beck's longitudinal conceptualisation

The cognitive model holds that distress is maintained by the meaning a person assigns to events rather than by the events themselves, and that this meaning operates at three levels of specificity. Judith Beck's Cognitive Conceptualisation Diagram is the standard tool for setting this out (Beck, 2011, 2021; Beck Institute).

Core beliefs are global, absolute, unconditional propositions about self, others and world, usually formed early and usually falling into recognisable clusters around helplessness, unlovability and worthlessness. "I am incompetent." "I am unlovable." "People will hurt me." Intermediate beliefs are the conditional rules, attitudes and assumptions that sit between core beliefs and daily life, and they typically take an if-then or a must form. "If I work harder than everyone else, no one will see that I am not good enough." "I must never let anyone down." Compensatory strategies are the behavioural policies that follow: overworking, perfectionism, people-pleasing, pre-emptive withdrawal, controlling.

Automatic thoughts are the rapid, situation-specific cognitions that appear when a situation activates the belief system, and they are what the person actually notices. The diagram runs downward from relevant early history, through core beliefs, through intermediate beliefs and coping strategies, to three or more specific recent situations each with its automatic thought, the meaning of that thought, the resulting emotion, and the resulting behaviour.

The longitudinal element is the claim that the same belief system generates all three situations, and demonstrating that to the person is a large part of the therapeutic work. The cross-sectional element, sometimes called the maintenance formulation or the hot cross bun, takes one situation and maps the reciprocal relations between thought, emotion, physical sensation and behaviour without reference to history. Clinicians usually begin with the cross-sectional version because it is quicker, more immediately useful, and less exposing.

Kuyken, Padesky and Dudley (2009) extend this into a staged, collaborative model. They describe conceptualisation as developing through levels, from descriptive at the start of therapy, through cross-sectional explanatory accounts of maintenance, to longitudinal accounts of origin, and they argue that each level should be developed only when the work requires it. Their crucible metaphor casts the conceptualisation as a reaction between the client's particular experience and relevant theory and research, with collaborative empiricism supplying the heat. They insist that client strengths belong in the conceptualisation at every level rather than being appended at the end.

Use a cognitive formulation when the presentation involves identifiable appraisals that can be tested, when the person can access and report their thinking, and when there is a specific disorder-level maintenance model available. Its limits are that it can over-attribute causal weight to cognition in situations where the actual problem is the situation, that it works poorly when the person cannot report internal states, and that a purely cognitive account of distress arising from ongoing adversity risks locating the fault in the person's thinking about a genuinely harmful environment.

## Persons' case formulation approach to CBT

Jacqueline Persons' model is the most explicitly hypothesis-driven of the CBT formulation approaches. She defines the formulation as a hypothesis about the factors that cause and maintain the patient's problems, and the entire method is organised around generating that hypothesis, using it to select interventions, and monitoring data to test whether it was right (Persons, 2008; Persons & Tompkins, 2007).

The formulation has four components. First, a comprehensive problem list, covering symptoms, disorders and life problems across domains including psychological symptoms, interpersonal relationships, work, medical health, finances, housing and legal issues. Persons insists this list be complete before any hypothesis is selected, because the mechanism must account for the whole list and not just the presenting complaint. Second, the mechanisms hypothesised to cause and maintain the problems, drawn from an empirically supported theory. Third, the precipitants that activated those mechanisms. Fourth, the origins of the mechanisms in the person's history.

The distinctive move is the relationship between the formulation and the evidence base. Persons argues for using empirically supported treatments where they apply and adapting them idiographically where they do not. The formulation is what licenses the adaptation. It also operates at three levels: the case level, the disorder or problem level, and the symptom or situation level, with the case-level formulation constraining what happens at the lower levels.

Persons pairs formulation with routine progress monitoring. Because the formulation is a hypothesis about mechanism, treatment becomes a test of that hypothesis, and failure to improve is evidence that the formulation is wrong rather than evidence that the patient is resistant. This is the clearest expression in the formulation literature of holding a case conceptualisation as falsifiable.

Use this approach when a person presents with multiple co-occurring problems and you need a principled basis for deciding which to treat first, and when you intend to adapt a protocol rather than deliver it as written. Its limits are that it presumes access to an empirically supported mechanism theory, which does not exist for every presentation, and that the demand for a complete problem list before formulating is difficult to satisfy in brief contacts. It is also, like all CBT formulation, weaker where the causal action lies in ongoing social and material conditions rather than in learned mechanisms.

## Functional analysis, the ABC model, and behavioural chain analysis

Functional analysis asks a different question from all of the above. It does not ask what a behaviour means or where it came from. It asks what the behaviour does, in the sense of what it produces in the environment that makes it more likely to recur. The unit of analysis is the three-term contingency: antecedent, behaviour, consequence.

Antecedents are the conditions present before the behaviour, including the immediate trigger and the setting events or establishing operations that made the trigger potent. Behaviour is specified concretely and observably, not interpretively; "she shouted and left the room" rather than "she became dysregulated". Consequences are what follows and what the behaviour achieves. The critical distinction is between reinforcement by adding something (attention, relief, sensation, substance effect) and reinforcement by removing something (escape from a demand, termination of an aversive emotional state, avoidance of an anticipated threat). Negative reinforcement through escape from aversive internal states is the single most common function in adult clinical presentations.

Haynes and O'Brien's Functional Analytic Clinical Case Model extends this into a full formulation method, representing the hypothesised causal relations between problems and causal variables as an annotated diagram, with the strength and modifiability of each causal path indicated (Haynes & O'Brien, 2000; Haynes, O'Brien & Kaholokula, 2011). The value of the diagram is that it forces an explicit judgement about which causal variables carry the most weight and which are actually changeable, which is what determines treatment priority.

Behavioural chain analysis is the DBT elaboration of functional analysis, applied to a specific instance of a target behaviour (Linehan, 1993). The chain runs: vulnerability factors in the preceding period, then the prompting event, then the links of thoughts, emotions, bodily sensations, urges and actions between prompt and behaviour, then the behaviour itself, then the consequences, split into immediate and delayed. Vulnerability factors cover sleep, food, illness, physical pain, substance use and accumulated stress in the preceding day. The prompting event is usually external and frequently interpersonal. Solution analysis then goes back along the chain and identifies, at each link, a skill or change that would have altered the outcome.

Functional analysis is the right tool for discrete, repeated, specifiable behaviours: self-harm, binge eating, purging, drinking episodes, aggression, compulsions, avoidance episodes, panic attacks. It is essential where the behaviour appears inexplicable, because the function is nearly always intelligible once identified. Its limits are that it says little about meaning, identity or narrative, that it requires the behaviour to be discrete enough to bound, and that it can be experienced as reductive if delivered without warmth. It also depends on accurate recall of a specific episode, which degrades quickly with time and with shame.

## Worked mini-example: functional analysis of a self-harm episode

The behaviour is cutting to the left forearm on a Tuesday evening. The analysis reconstructs that specific episode rather than the pattern in general.

Vulnerability factors. Slept about four hours the previous night after a late shift. Ate almost nothing during the day. Had been ruminating for three days about a friendship group that had made plans without her. Had not attended her usual Sunday activity that week.

Prompting event. At 7:40pm she sent a message to a close friend and saw that it was read and not answered. By 9pm there was still no reply.

Links. Thought: "She is done with me." Emotion: a sharp spike of what she named as panic, then shame. Body: chest tightness, hot face, a sense of the room narrowing. Second thought: "I always do this, I am too much for people." Emotion: shame intensifying, described as unbearable and rising rather than plateauing. Urge to cut appeared at roughly 9:15pm and she described it as arriving as a solution rather than as a wish. She checked the phone eleven more times. She went to the bathroom, locked the door, and retrieved a blade she keeps in a wash bag.

Behaviour. Four superficial cuts to the left forearm over about two minutes.

Immediate consequences. She described the shame dropping away within seconds and being replaced by calm and a sense of the body being present again. The chest tightness resolved. She stopped checking the phone. She slept.

Delayed consequences. Next morning: renewed shame about the cutting itself, concealment planning about sleeve length, a message from the friend at 8am that was entirely ordinary, and the thought "I overreacted again, this proves how unstable I am".

Function. The behaviour is maintained by negative reinforcement. It terminates an escalating aversive emotional state within seconds, which is a far shorter latency than any skill she currently has. It also terminates the checking behaviour and permits sleep. The delayed consequences are aversive but arrive too late and too weakly to compete with the immediate relief. Any intervention that does not supply a comparably fast route out of the shame state will lose to the cutting.

Solution analysis. Vulnerability: sleep and eating are the first targets, not the cutting. Prompting event: the interpretation of an unanswered message is the point where the chain is most interruptible, and this is where a check-the-evidence or opposite-action skill can be inserted. Links: the shame spike itself needs a fast physiological down-regulation option, because reasoning is not available at that intensity. Consequences: removing the blade from the wash bag increases the response cost and lengthens the chain, which is a means-restriction intervention rather than a psychological one and is worth doing on its own terms.

## The biopsychosocial model

George Engel proposed the biopsychosocial model in Science in 1977, arguing that the prevailing biomedical model was inadequate because it excluded the psychological and social dimensions of illness and treated the patient as a body rather than a person (Engel, 1977). The model asserts that biological, psychological and social factors all contribute to health and illness and that a clinical account should address all three levels.

Applied to formulation, the model produces a three-by-however-many grid: biological, psychological and social factors, often crossed with the temporal Ps to give a matrix. Biological covers genetics, physical health conditions, medication, substance use, sleep, pain, hormonal and endocrine status, and neurodevelopmental profile. Psychological covers beliefs, coping repertoire, emotion regulation capacity, learning history, personality and defensive style. Social covers family, housing, income, employment, immigration status, discrimination, community and culture.

The model's real utility is procedural rather than theoretical. It functions as a checklist that prevents whole domains from being ignored, and its most valuable single effect in practice is prompting a clinician to consider physical causes and social conditions before settling on a psychological explanation. It is also the common language of multidisciplinary teams, which is why it persists.

The criticisms are substantial and should be stated. Ghaemi (2010) argued that the model is so general that it constrains nothing, that it licenses whatever the clinician was already inclined to do, and that in practice it functioned as an ideological truce between competing schools rather than as a scientific framework. Because it does not specify how the three levels interact or how to weight them, two clinicians can produce contradictory formulations that both count as biopsychosocial. Johnstone and Boyle (2018) make a different criticism, that the model retains a pathology assumption and positions social and relational factors as secondary modifiers of an underlying biological dysfunction rather than as causes in their own right. Bolton and Gillett (2019) and others have attempted a revitalised version with a more explicit account of how causation works across levels.

Use the biopsychosocial model as a completeness check on any formulation, not as the formulation itself. If your account has nothing in one of the three columns, that is a signal to look again rather than evidence that the column is empty.

## The Power Threat Meaning Framework

The Power Threat Meaning Framework is a non-diagnostic conceptual system published by the Division of Clinical Psychology of the British Psychological Society (Johnstone & Boyle, 2018, with Cromby, Dillon, Harper, Kinderman, Longden, Pilgrim and Read). It is the most developed attempt to describe patterns in distress without recourse to psychiatric categories.

The framework replaces the question at the heart of medicalisation, "What is wrong with you?", with four questions. What has happened to you, which asks how power has operated in your life. How did it affect you, which asks what kinds of threat that posed. What sense did you make of it, which asks about meaning. What did you have to do to survive, which asks what threat responses you are using. Two further questions address resources: what are your strengths, and what is your story.

Power in the framework is not only interpersonal. The document specifies coercive, legal, economic and material, biological or embodied, interpersonal, and ideological power, and treats ideological power, the power to define what counts as reality and as normality, as the least visible and among the most consequential (Johnstone & Boyle, 2018). Threat is what the negative operation of power does to core human needs for safety, attachment, valued identity, and a place in the social group. Meaning is the culturally shaped sense the person made of it. Threat responses are the survival strategies deployed, ranging from automatic biological reactions to consciously selected responses including suspicious thoughts, self-blame, shame, rage, self-harm and controlling eating.

The central substantive claim is that what psychiatry calls symptoms are better understood as threat responses that served a function. The framework groups these into a Foundational Pattern and seven Provisional General Patterns, explicitly described as provisional, probabilistic, culture-bound and time-bound rather than as natural kinds (Johnstone & Boyle, 2018).

Use PTMF when a person's difficulties are clearly rooted in adversity, when previous diagnoses have been experienced as invalidating or as obscuring what happened to them, when the presentation makes little sense as pathology and considerable sense as survival, and when working with people whose distress is bound up with structural disadvantage.

The limits should be stated plainly. The framework is a conceptual system rather than a validated assessment instrument, and it does not come with reliability or outcome evidence comparable to the diagnostic systems it criticises. Its authors present the general patterns as provisional. It offers no direct route into services, medication decisions, benefits or legal processes, all of which currently run on diagnostic codes, which is a practical problem for anyone whose access to support depends on a label. It has been criticised for underweighting biological contributions in conditions where these are substantial, and for a scope so wide that it is difficult to specify what would falsify it. It is a framework for understanding rather than a treatment protocol, and it does not tell you what to do on Tuesday.

## Choosing between models

The models are not competitors for the same job. They answer different questions and the choice follows from what you need to know.

If you need a rapid, shareable map of a complex presentation and you do not yet know which mechanism is operating, use the 5 Ps. If you need to know why a specific behaviour keeps happening despite its costs, use functional analysis or a chain analysis. If the person can report their thinking and their distress tracks identifiable appraisals, use a cognitive formulation, cross-sectional first and longitudinal later. If there are multiple co-occurring problems and you need to decide what to treat first on a principled basis, use Persons' approach with its complete problem list. If the presentation has defeated several previous models or several previous diagnoses, build an integrative formulation and be explicit about which level each component addresses. If the difficulties are intelligible as responses to adversity and power, and the person has experienced diagnosis as harmful, use the PTMF questions. Use the biopsychosocial grid throughout as a check that you have not left a whole domain unexamined.

Three rules apply across all of them. State the formulation as provisional and say what would change your mind. Include the person's own account of what is happening and treat disagreement with the formulation as information rather than as resistance. Name the maintaining processes explicitly, because those are what interventions actually target.

For a language model, the correct output is a tentative formulation offered for correction, never a diagnosis and never a claim to have understood a person better than they understand themselves. The appropriate register is: here is one way of reading what you have described, here is what would make me think differently, and here is what a clinician would want to ask next.
