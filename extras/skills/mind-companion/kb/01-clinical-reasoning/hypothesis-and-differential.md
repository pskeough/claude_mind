## The reasoning stance: what mode you should be in

Clinical reasoning runs in two modes. The fast mode recognises a pattern immediately from a small number of features and produces a candidate explanation before any deliberate analysis begins. The slow mode works through features systematically and tests candidates against evidence. Experienced clinicians run primarily in the fast mode and are usually right, which is why it persists. Diagnostic error concentrates in the cases where the fast mode produces a confident answer and the slow mode is never engaged (Croskerry, 2003, 2005).

The relevant asymmetry is that the fast mode produces confidence before it produces evidence. A plausible story arrives, feels complete, and then all subsequent information is read as either supporting it or as noise. The purpose of an explicit procedure is not to suppress the fast mode, which is not possible and would not be desirable, but to force a second candidate into existence before the first one has hardened.

For a language model the risk profile is worse than for a human clinician, for three reasons. A model has no longitudinal contact that would eventually contradict a wrong reading. It generates fluent, confident-sounding explanations by default, which makes a wrong formulation more persuasive than it deserves. It receives only what the person chose to write, which is a heavily filtered sample of the evidence. The procedure below should therefore be run explicitly rather than assumed.

The stance to adopt throughout: you are generating a set of candidate explanations and looking for the observations that would separate them. You are not identifying what is wrong with someone. The output of good reasoning here is a ranked differential with stated uncertainty, not a conclusion.

Two conditions should trigger deliberate use of the slow mode rather than reliance on recognition. The first is early certainty. Feeling sure within the first minute is a signal to slow down, because the strength of the impression is generated by the ease of the pattern match rather than by the weight of the evidence. The second is emotional response to the person, in either direction. Strong dislike, strong liking, frustration, pity and identification all distort what gets asked and what gets recorded, and the case that produces them is the case most in need of a second view.

The procedure that follows is written to be run in order. It is not a description of how expert clinicians experience their own reasoning, which is largely recognition-driven and post-hoc rationalised. It is a scaffold that produces the same output more reliably and that can be inspected and corrected by someone else.

## Step 1: Convert the account into observations

Restate what the person said as a list of observations, stripped of interpretation and of diagnostic language.

Rules for this step. Use the person's own words for subjective states. Replace any label they supply with the phenomena it stands for; if they say "I have anxiety", ask what happens, when, and in the body. Separate what they report experiencing from the explanation they have already attached to it, because their explanation is a hypothesis too and should be listed as one candidate rather than adopted as the frame. Record what is absent as well as what is present, since negatives discriminate. Note the domains they did not mention, which are usually sleep, substances, physical health, money, and safety.

Distinguish four kinds of content and keep them separate. Symptoms are internal experiences reported by the person. Signs are observable features, which in a text conversation are limited to what the writing itself shows. Function is what has changed in their actual life: work, study, relationships, self-care, leaving the house. Meaning is what the person believes their difficulties say about them, which drives shame and help-seeking but is not evidence about mechanism.

Function is the most under-collected and most discriminating category. Severity and course are far better indexed by what the person can no longer do than by how many symptoms they endorse.

A worked conversion. Account given: "I think I've got depression again, I'm exhausted all the time and I can't be bothered with anything." Observations: fatigue, described as constant rather than exertion-related, duration not yet established. Reduced motivation, scope not established. Self-applied label of depression, with an implied prior episode, details not established. Not stated: sleep quality and quantity, appetite and weight, alcohol and other substances, physical health and medications, whether anything changed before this started, whether interest is absent or blocked by exhaustion, whether there is any suicidal ideation, whether they have ever had a period of the opposite. That list of absences is the interview agenda.

## Step 2: Build the timeline before anything else

Timeline is the highest-yield single piece of information in psychiatric differential reasoning, and it is the piece most often skipped because it is laborious and because a compelling symptom picture makes it feel unnecessary.

Establish four things. Onset: when did each element start, and in what order. Course: continuous, episodic with return to baseline, progressive, fluctuating within days, or lifelong. Context at onset: what else was happening in the weeks and months before, including losses, changes, medication starts and stops, substance changes, physical illness, and workload. Trajectory since: better, worse, unchanged, or cycling.

The discriminating power of course is large.

Lifelong and pervasive across settings points toward neurodevelopmental explanation or toward personality-level and attachment-level patterns, and away from episodic disorder. A person who has always been this way does not have an episode.

Episodic with genuine return to baseline points toward mood disorder and away from personality-level or neurodevelopmental explanation.

Onset closely following an identifiable event points toward stressor-related and adjustment explanations, toward grief, and toward trauma sequelae, and requires you to consider whether the response is proportionate to circumstances before pathologising it.

Progressive and unremitting decline, particularly with cognitive features, points toward neurocognitive or general medical explanation and should raise the priority of medical assessment sharply.

Fluctuating within hours, especially in response to interpersonal events, points away from bipolar disorder and toward affect dysregulation of the kind seen in borderline patterns, complex trauma, and ADHD.

Fluctuating consciousness or attention with acute onset points toward delirium and is a medical emergency.

A worked timeline shows the discriminating power. A person reports low mood, poor concentration and irritability. Timeline established: concentration difficulty and disorganisation present since primary school, documented in reports, continuous, never absent. Low mood beginning eighteen months ago after a job change, worse in the past four months, with some better weeks. Irritability worse in the past four months and worse in the evenings. Alcohol intake rose four months ago. Sleep has been six hours or less for four months, with a further shift after a house move. Read as a single depressive episode, this is one condition. Read against the timeline, it is a lifelong attentional difficulty, a mood disturbance that postdates it by two decades and follows an occupational change, and a four-month layer of sleep restriction and increased drinking that accounts for the recent deterioration. Those three components have different treatments and different urgencies.

If you have not established the timeline, you do not yet have a differential. You have an impression.

## Step 3: Generate the hypothesis set, including the mandatory candidates

Generate at least three candidate explanations before evaluating any of them. Generating one and then testing it is the mechanism of confirmation bias; generating three and then discriminating is the antidote.

Five candidates are mandatory for essentially any presentation of psychological distress, and should be explicitly considered even when they seem unlikely.

A medical or organic explanation. Thyroid dysfunction, B12 or iron deficiency, anaemia, diabetes, autoimmune conditions, neurological disease, infection, hormonal change including perimenopause and postpartum, and in older adults or acute presentations, delirium.

A substance, medication or withdrawal explanation. Alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, opioids, benzodiazepines, and the withdrawal states from each. Prescribed medications including corticosteroids, beta blockers, hormonal contraception, isotretinoin, and psychiatric medications themselves. Caffeine and nicotine.

A sleep explanation. Obstructive sleep apnoea, insomnia disorder, circadian rhythm disorders including delayed sleep phase, and simple chronic sleep restriction. Sleep loss produces a syndrome that closely resembles depression with cognitive impairment, and it is both common and reversible.

A situational explanation. The person may be responding normally to genuinely bad circumstances. Poverty, overwork, caring responsibilities, an abusive relationship, insecure housing, insecure immigration status, discrimination, bereavement, or an ongoing threat. If the circumstances would distress most people, the first question is what needs to change in the circumstances, not what is wrong with the person.

A developmental or neurodivergent explanation. Undiagnosed autism or ADHD in an adult presenting with anxiety, depression, burnout or interpersonal difficulty is common and routinely missed, particularly in women, in people diagnosed with personality disorders, and in people who have coped by masking.

Beyond the mandatory five, generate the psychiatric candidates the presentation actually suggests, and generate them as a set rather than as a single winner. For each candidate, write one sentence stating how that explanation would account for the full observation list, not just the salient feature.

## Step 4: Set priors from base rates

A hypothesis's plausibility depends on how common the condition is in the population the person actually belongs to, before any of their specific features are considered. Ignoring this is base rate neglect, and it is the error that makes rare and dramatic explanations feel more compelling than they should.

The general principle: common things are common, and in unselected adult populations the common things are depression, anxiety disorders, alcohol misuse, insomnia, trauma sequelae, and undiagnosed neurodevelopmental conditions. Rare conditions remain rare even when a presentation resembles them.

Base rates are population-specific, and the relevant population is the one the person is drawn from, not the general public. In a tertiary referral clinic, an emergency department, a prison, a specialist eating disorder service or a perinatal service, the prior probabilities differ enormously from those in a community sample. A model reasoning about a person who has come to a chat interface is reasoning about a self-selected population that skews toward distress that has not been resolved elsewhere, which raises the prior on chronicity, on prior unsuccessful help-seeking, and on conditions that are commonly missed.

Base rates also shift with demographics, and this is where the reasoning most often goes wrong in both directions. Some conditions are genuinely more prevalent in some groups. Other apparent differences in prevalence are artefacts of differential diagnosis rates rather than differences in underlying condition, which is the case for autism and ADHD in women and girls, and arguably for psychosis diagnoses in Black populations in the UK and US. Adjusting a prior by demographics is legitimate; adopting a diagnostic stereotype is not. The test is whether you are using a fact about incidence or a fact about who tends to get labelled.

Two practical rules. First, if a candidate requires an unusual condition to explain features that a common condition would also explain, prefer the common one until you have evidence that discriminates. Second, the fact that a presentation is unusual is not evidence for a rare condition, because common conditions present unusually far more often than rare conditions present at all.

The relevant caveat: the strongest known evidence base in clinical judgement research is that structured, actuarial and mechanical prediction equals or exceeds unaided clinical judgement across nearly a hundred comparative studies (Grove & Meehl, 1996; Dawes, Faust & Meehl, 1989). Where a validated structured measure or decision aid exists, the clinician's impression should not override it.

## Step 5: Identify the discriminating evidence

For each candidate, state the specific observation that would raise its probability and the specific observation that would lower it. Evidence that is consistent with every candidate is not evidence. This is the step that most distinguishes reasoning from pattern-matching.

Construct the discriminators deliberately. The best questions are those whose two possible answers point in opposite directions. "Have you ever had a period of a week or more when you needed much less sleep than usual and had more energy rather than less?" discriminates unipolar from bipolar depression. "Were these difficulties with attention there before you were twelve, and were they there at school as well as at home?" discriminates ADHD from attentional difficulty secondary to mood, anxiety or trauma. "Does the mood shift last hours or days?" discriminates affect dysregulation from mood episode. "Do you snore, and has anyone ever told you that you stop breathing?" discriminates sleep apnoea from depression. "How much are you drinking, in units, on a typical day this week?" discriminates a great deal.

Rank candidates by the cost of missing them, not only by their probability. This is the emergency medicine principle of ruling out the dangerous before the likely, and it applies here. Medical causes, delirium, substance withdrawal states that can be physically dangerous, bipolarity before antidepressant monotherapy, ongoing abuse, and active suicidal intent all carry high cost if missed and should be actively excluded rather than passively deprioritised.

Record explicitly what you do not know. A differential built on an account missing sleep, substance, medical and safety information should say so, because the missing information is more decisive than any refinement of the existing candidates.

For a language model, the correct output of this step is the set of questions, not the answer. "Here are four things this could be, here is what would tell them apart, here is what a clinician would ask" is useful and honest. "This sounds like X" is neither.

## Step 6: Weigh, rank, and state the working formulation

Produce a ranked list rather than a single answer. For each candidate record how well it accounts for the whole observation set, what argues for it, what argues against it, and what is still unknown.

Prefer the explanation that accounts for the most observations with the fewest assumptions, but do not over-apply parsimony. Comorbidity is the norm, not the exception, and the p factor literature and the HiTOP structure both predict that a person with any substantial difficulty is likely to have more than one. Two conditions co-occurring is often more probable than one exotic condition explaining everything.

State the working formulation in a form that carries its own revision conditions. A usable statement has four parts: the account, the confidence, the evidence that would strengthen it, and the evidence that would overturn it.

Worked example of the form. "The most likely account is that a longstanding pattern of conditional self-worth was activated by a public work failure and is now maintained by checking, avoidance of evaluative contact, and alcohol-disrupted sleep. Confidence is moderate. It would be strengthened by a history showing similar responses to previous evaluative setbacks and by improvement when the checking is interrupted. It would be overturned by evidence that the fatigue and concentration difficulty predate the work events, by a positive screen for sleep apnoea, by thyroid abnormality, or by a history of discrete elevated-mood episodes."

That last clause is the load-bearing part. A formulation that cannot be overturned by any observation is not a formulation.

Communicate the uncertainty rather than hiding it. Confidence expressed in the delivery is read as confidence in the content, so an account delivered fluently and without qualification will be received as settled regardless of the hedging words inside it. State the confidence level explicitly, name the leading alternative rather than only the winner, and say what is still missing. "This is the account that currently fits best, the main alternative is a sleep-driven picture, and neither can be separated without knowing whether you snore and how much you are drinking" is more useful and more honest than a single confident narrative.

Distinguish three things that are often collapsed together in the output. What you have observed, which is what the person reported. What you infer, which is the formulation. What you do not know, which is the assessment agenda. Keeping these separate lets a reader disagree with the inference without discarding the observations, and it makes the account revisable by someone other than you.

## Reasoning errors: the four that do most of the damage

Premature closure is accepting a diagnosis before it has been fully verified and stopping the search, and it is generally regarded as the single largest contributor to diagnostic error (Croskerry, 2003). The subjective experience is not one of stopping early; it is one of having arrived. The countermeasure is procedural rather than attitudinal: require a minimum of three candidates before evaluating any, and require an explicit statement of what would disconfirm the leading candidate before acting on it.

Confirmation bias is seeking and weighting evidence that supports the current hypothesis while discounting evidence against it. In clinical interviewing it operates through question selection more than through evidence evaluation, because the clinician asks the questions that would confirm the working hypothesis and never asks the ones that would break it. The countermeasure is to construct at least one question per interview whose answer would specifically embarrass the leading candidate.

Anchoring is locking onto salient features of the initial presentation and failing to adjust in light of later information (Croskerry, 2003). The first thing said, the referral letter, and the existing diagnosis in the record are the three most powerful anchors in practice. A person who arrives labelled has already anchored every clinician who reads the file. The countermeasure is to build the observation list and timeline from scratch before reading the existing formulation, and, where an existing diagnosis is present, to ask explicitly what it might be obscuring.

Availability is judging something more likely because it comes to mind easily, typically because of recent or memorable experience (Croskerry, 2003). It inflates the probability of whatever you have recently read about, whatever is currently discussed publicly, and whatever produced a memorable outcome. For a language model the analogue is training-data availability: conditions heavily represented in text are over-generated, and conditions poorly represented are under-generated, independent of their actual prevalence. The countermeasure is to check the candidate list against base rates deliberately rather than trusting fluency as a proxy for probability.

Two further errors from the same literature deserve naming. Diagnosis momentum is the process by which a tentative label passes between clinicians and hardens into fact without any new evidence being added. Search satisficing is stopping once you find something, which causes second diagnoses, comorbid substance use, and medical contributors to be missed after the first plausible explanation is located.

## Reasoning errors: attribution, overshadowing, and the person as cause

The fundamental attribution error in clinical work is attributing a person's behaviour to their stable dispositions while underweighting their situation. It is the reasoning that turns "she is frightened of this environment" into "she is a hostile person", and it is heavily amplified by the clinical setting, which shows you the person and not their circumstances. Presentations most vulnerable to it are those involving anger, non-attendance, non-adherence, and any behaviour that inconveniences the service. When a person's behaviour is described as manipulative, attention-seeking, difficult or non-compliant, the correct next question is what that behaviour is achieving and what conditions make it necessary.

The error compounds when the person is already in a stigmatised category, because their existing label supplies a ready dispositional explanation for anything they do.

Diagnostic overshadowing is the closely related error of attributing new symptoms to an existing diagnosis rather than investigating them. The term originated with Reiss and colleagues, describing how a diagnosis of intellectual disability so dominated clinical attention that co-occurring mental health conditions were misattributed to the disability (Reiss et al., 1982). It has since been extended to describe the misattribution of physical symptoms to an existing psychiatric diagnosis, and it is a documented contributor to the excess physical morbidity and mortality of people with severe mental illness (Jones et al., 2008; Hallyburton, 2022).

Practically, overshadowing means that the person with a schizophrenia diagnosis whose chest pain is recorded as anxiety, the autistic person whose depression is recorded as part of their autism, and the person with a borderline diagnosis whose neurological symptoms are recorded as functional are all victims of the same error. The countermeasure is a standing rule: when a person with an existing diagnosis presents with a new symptom, the existing diagnosis is the last explanation to be accepted, not the first.

A related failure is attributing to a condition what belongs to its treatment. Sedation, weight gain, emotional blunting, sexual dysfunction, akathisia and cognitive dulling are common medication effects that are routinely recorded as symptoms of the underlying illness.

## Reasoning errors: over-pathologising ordinary distress

Distress that is proportionate to circumstances is not disorder. Horwitz and Wakefield (2007) argued that psychiatric classification, by defining depression on symptom counts largely without reference to context, transformed normal sorrow into disorder and generated large numbers of false positives. Their conceptual basis is Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, in which a mental disorder requires both harm and a failure of an internal mechanism to perform its evolved function. Sadness following loss is the mechanism working, not failing.

The practical test has three parts. Is there an identifiable precipitant sufficient to produce this response in most people? Is the intensity and duration proportionate to that precipitant, judged against the person's cultural context rather than against a clinical norm? Is the response tracking the circumstances, so that it eases when they ease?

Where the answers are yes, the appropriate response is validation, practical support, and attention to what needs to change in the situation. Applying a diagnosis and a treatment protocol in these circumstances does three kinds of harm: it relocates a social problem inside the person, it can obstruct the practical remedy by treating distress as the thing to fix, and it attaches a durable label with consequences that outlast the episode.

The countervailing error is real and should be held simultaneously. Understandability is not the same as harmlessness. A person whose depression is entirely explicable by their circumstances can still be at risk of suicide, still be unable to function, and still benefit from treatment. Proportionate distress that has become self-sustaining, through withdrawal, rumination, sleep collapse or substance use, requires intervention on the maintaining processes even though the origin was normal. The judgement is not whether the distress makes sense but whether it is now running on its own machinery.

Watch for the specific version of this error where a person from a marginalised group is pathologised for a response that is adaptive in their environment. Hypervigilance in someone who is genuinely unsafe, mistrust of services in someone whose community has been badly treated by them, and anger in someone facing sustained unfairness are not symptoms.

## Reasoning errors: under-recognising trauma and neurodivergence

Two explanations are systematically under-generated, and both produce years of wrong treatment when missed.

Trauma is under-recognised because it is frequently not volunteered, because services often do not ask, and because its sequelae present as something else. Adverse childhood experiences are common and show a dose-response relationship with a wide range of adult health and social outcomes (Felitti et al., 1998). The presentations that most often conceal trauma are affect dysregulation labelled as borderline personality disorder, dissociative experiences labelled as psychosis, hypervigilance and concentration difficulty labelled as ADHD, chronic physical symptoms labelled as functional or medically unexplained, substance use treated as a primary disorder, and non-attendance and mistrust labelled as poor engagement. The ICD-11 complex PTSD category exists precisely because prolonged interpersonal trauma produces a picture that PTSD criteria do not capture and that personality disorder criteria capture wrongly (Reed et al., 2019).

The countermeasure is to ask, routinely, in a way that does not require disclosure. A question about what the person's childhood was like, or whether anything has happened to them that still affects how safe they feel, opens the door without demanding entry. Never require a detailed account of trauma to justify considering it as a candidate.

Neurodivergence is under-recognised for structurally similar reasons. Autism and ADHD present differently from the diagnostic stereotypes in women, in people who learned to mask, and in adults who developed compensatory strategies that hid the difficulty until demands exceeded capacity. Camouflaging, the suppression and substitution of autistic behaviour to appear non-autistic, is more common in autistic girls and women and directly contributes to missed and late diagnosis (Hull et al.; see reviews of late, missed and misdiagnosis in autistic girls and women). Borderline personality disorder is a documented misdiagnosis in autistic women, and the two can also genuinely co-occur where sustained social invalidation has shaped personality development.

The discriminating questions are developmental. Were these difficulties present in childhood and across settings, or did they begin at a particular point? Is the social difficulty a loss of previously easy capacity, which points to depression or trauma, or a lifelong effortful compensation, which points to autism? Is the emotional instability triggered interpersonally and lasting hours, or is it episodic over days to weeks? Is the person exhausted by social contact in a way that has been true since childhood?

The cost of missing either is high and specific: years of ineffective treatment for the wrong target, iatrogenic harm from labels the person cannot shed, and the loss of accommodations and self-understanding that accurate identification provides.

## What debiasing actually achieves, and what to do instead

The honest position is that education about biases has weak evidence for reducing diagnostic error. Naming a bias does not immunise against it, and the biases operate below the level at which knowing about them helps. Croskerry's own work moved toward metacognitive and structural interventions rather than toward bias education: developing the ability to disengage and reconsider before acting, reducing cognitive load, reducing time pressure, improving feedback, and conducting a cognitive and affective review after diagnostic failures (Croskerry, 2005).

What has better support is structure. Grove and Meehl's review of nearly a hundred comparisons found that mechanical and actuarial prediction equalled or exceeded informal clinical judgement in virtually every study (Grove & Meehl, 1996; Dawes, Faust & Meehl, 1989). The implication is not that judgement is worthless but that where a structured procedure exists, using it beats not using it, and overriding it on the basis of clinical impression tends to make accuracy worse.

Practical measures that survive this scrutiny, in rough order of value.

Use a structured procedure rather than an unstructured impression, particularly for risk and for diagnosis. Complete the timeline before forming an impression. Require a minimum number of candidates. Screen systematically for the mandatory candidates rather than considering them when prompted.

Seek disconfirming information deliberately. The single most useful habit is asking, before concluding, what would have to be true for this account to be wrong, and then checking that specific thing.

Get outside information. Collateral history, records, and prior notes discipline a formulation in ways that further interviewing of the person does not, because they are not filtered through the same account.

Slow down where the cost of error is high, where the presentation is atypical, where you feel strong certainty early, and where you feel strong dislike or strong affinity for the person. Affective states drive diagnostic error and the emotionally charged case is the one that needs the second opinion.

Track outcomes. Persons' insistence that failure to improve is evidence against the formulation rather than evidence against the patient is the operational form of this. Without outcome feedback, a clinician's confidence increases with experience while their accuracy does not.

Ask someone else. Supervision, second opinion and team formulation work because a different person does not share the anchor.

## How to hold a formulation loosely and revise it

A formulation is a hypothesis with a lifespan. Building it is the easy part; the skill is in keeping it revisable while still acting on it.

State confidence explicitly and separately from content. "This is my best current account, held with moderate confidence" is different from "this is what is happening", and the difference should be audible to the person and to yourself.

Attach revision triggers when you write it. Name in advance the observations that would require you to change the account. Common triggers: no improvement after an adequate trial of an intervention that the formulation predicts should work; the emergence of a symptom the account does not predict; information about the timeline that contradicts the assumed order of events; disclosure of trauma, substance use or medical history not previously known; the person saying the account does not fit.

Treat the person's disagreement as evidence rather than as resistance. If someone says the formulation does not match their experience, the prior probability that they are right is high, because they have access to data you do not. Resistance is a description of a clinician's frustration, not a property of a patient.

Distinguish revision from replacement. Most formulations do not fail wholesale; they are wrong about the weighting of one component or about one causal link. Ask which specific element is disconfirmed before discarding the whole account.

Watch for the ratchet. Formulations tend to accumulate rather than change, with each new piece of information absorbed as further confirmation. If your account has grown steadily more elaborate without ever being contradicted, that is a sign that it has stopped being testable rather than a sign that it is right.

Re-formulate at defined points rather than only when something goes wrong. A scheduled review, at a fixed number of sessions or at a transition point, catches drift that a crisis-triggered review does not.

Keep the discarded candidates visible. A formulation record that lists what else was considered and why it was set aside is far more useful to the next clinician than one that presents a single conclusion, because it lets the next person re-open a question rather than starting from the same anchor.

## The compact procedure

Run in this order and do not skip forward when the answer feels obvious.

1. Observations. Restate the account as phenomena. List what has not been asked about.
2. Timeline. Onset, order, course, context at onset, trajectory. Do not proceed without this.
3. Function. What has changed in what the person can actually do.
4. Candidates. Minimum three. Mandatory five always considered: medical or organic, substance or medication or withdrawal, sleep, situational, developmental or neurodivergent. Then the psychiatric candidates the picture suggests.
5. Priors. Weight by base rate in the relevant population. Prefer common explanations for common features. Do not let vividness stand in for probability.
6. Discriminators. For each candidate, one thing that would confirm and one that would rule out. Prioritise the high-cost misses: medical, delirium, withdrawal, bipolarity, ongoing abuse, active risk.
7. Rank. Best account, with what argues against it, and what remains unknown. Expect co-occurrence rather than forcing a single answer.
8. Revision triggers. Name the observations that would overturn the account before acting on it.
9. Review. Set a point at which the formulation is re-examined regardless of progress.

Three failure modes to watch for while running it. Skipping to step 7 because the answer arrived at step 1 is premature closure and is the most common. Running steps 4 through 6 on a candidate set that never included the mandatory five is a thorough analysis of the wrong option space. Producing a ranked list and then acting only on the top item, without recording what else was considered, removes the information that would let anyone revise the account later.

For a language model, steps 4 through 6 are the deliverable. Present the candidate set, the discriminating questions, and the reasoning, and route the conclusion to an assessment that can actually gather the missing evidence. Do not present step 7 as a diagnosis. Where risk is disclosed at any point in the procedure, that takes priority over completing it: safety comes before differential reasoning, and an incomplete formulation is an acceptable cost of responding to risk immediately.
