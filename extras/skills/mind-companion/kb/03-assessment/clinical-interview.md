## What the assessment interview is for

The assessment interview has three jobs at once, and they compete. It gathers the information needed to build a formulation. It establishes enough of a working relationship that the person will keep talking. It begins the intervention, because being asked coherent questions by someone who is listening is itself a change in the person's situation.

Clinicians who treat the interview purely as data collection get thin data. People withhold the material that matters most when they feel processed. Clinicians who treat it purely as rapport-building arrive at the end of an hour with warmth and no history. The skill is running both tracks simultaneously, which means the structure lives in the clinician's head rather than on a clipboard.

The output of an assessment interview is not a diagnosis. It is a formulation: an account of what is happening, how it developed, what maintains it, and what might shift it. Diagnosis, where relevant, is a compressed summary of part of that account. A formulation that explains the person's difficulties in terms of predisposing, precipitating, perpetuating, and protective factors carries more clinical information than a diagnostic label, and it directly implies what to try (Johnstone & Dallos, 2013).

Assessment is not a phase that finishes. The first interview generates hypotheses. Later sessions test them. Information that changes the formulation arrives throughout treatment, often years in. Trauma history in particular is frequently disclosed late, after the person has tested whether the clinician can hold it. Treating the initial assessment as closed is a common error.

The domains below are what a competent assessment covers. The order is not fixed. A skilled interviewer moves between them following the person's material, and audits at the end for what has not been covered. What follows is a coverage checklist, not a script.

Coverage: presenting problem and its history; onset, course, and precipitants; previous episodes and previous treatment; current functioning across work, relationships, sleep, appetite, and substance use; risk; developmental history; family history; medical history and medications; strengths and supports; and the mental state examination.

## Opening the interview

The opening sets the frame for everything after it. Three things need to happen in the first few minutes: the person needs to know what this is, what happens to what they say, and that they can start wherever they want.

Orientation covers the practical shape of the meeting. How long it will run, what the clinician is trying to work out, and what happens at the end of it. People arrive with wildly different expectations of what an assessment is, including expectations of judgement or of being sectioned. Naming the shape of the hour removes some of that.

Confidentiality and its limits are stated before the person discloses, not after. The limits are specific: risk to self, risk to others, risk to a child, and legal compulsion. Stating them at the start is a fairness issue. A person who discloses suicidal intent and only then learns the clinician may have to act has been ambushed. Most people, told the limits plainly and without drama, disclose anyway.

The first substantive question should be open and should hand control to the person. "What brings you here?" and "Where would you like to start?" both work. "Tell me what has been happening" works when the referral already names a problem. What these have in common is that they do not presuppose the shape of the answer.

Then the clinician gets out of the way. Uninterrupted opening narrative is high-yield and cheap to obtain, and clinicians routinely fail to obtain it. In a secondary analysis of 112 recorded US clinical encounters, clinicians elicited the patient's agenda in only 40 of 112 encounters (36%), interrupted the patient in 27 of those 40 (67%), and the median time to interruption was 11 seconds. Where patients were allowed to finish, they completed their opening statement in a median of 6 seconds (Singh Ospina et al., 2019). The cost of letting someone finish is small and the cost of cutting them off is a reframed agenda. What the person chooses to say first, in what order, and what they leave out is itself assessment data.

In text, the opening changes in specific ways. There is no waiting room, no handshake, no visible clinician. The person may not know whether they are talking to a person, a service, or a model. That has to be stated. The invitation to start anywhere works the same, but the pause that lets someone gather themselves has no equivalent, so a text opening should explicitly signal that a long answer is welcome and that there is no rush.

## Presenting problem, onset, course, and precipitants

The presenting problem is what the person says is wrong, in their words, before it is translated into clinical categories. Record the words. "I can't get out of bed" and "I'm depressed" are different pieces of data even when they co-occur, and the first is more useful.

Onset asks when this started and how sharply. Insidious onset over months, acute onset over days, and onset the person cannot date at all point in different directions. An acute onset invites questions about what happened in the days before. A gradual onset that the person cannot date sometimes reflects a chronic state they have stopped noticing as abnormal, which is common in dysthymic presentations and in long-term consequences of childhood adversity.

Course asks the shape since onset. Steadily worsening, fluctuating, episodic with full recovery between episodes, or stable at a low level. Episodic course with complete inter-episode recovery raises different possibilities than an unremitting course. Seasonal patterning, menstrual patterning, and patterning that tracks a specific relationship or work cycle are worth asking about directly, because people rarely volunteer them.

Precipitants ask what was happening when it started. The useful question is not "what caused this" but "what was going on in your life around then". People frequently under-report precipitants either because they have normalised the event or because they believe the clinician wants a biological answer. Bereavement, relationship breakdown, job loss, migration, financial shock, illness, and anniversary reactions are the common ones. Absence of an identifiable precipitant is also data.

Severity needs anchoring to function rather than adjectives. "How bad is it" produces uninterpretable answers. "What are you not doing now that you were doing six months ago" produces usable ones. So does asking for a concrete recent day: what time did you wake, what did you do, who did you speak to.

Frequency and duration matter for anything episodic. Panic attacks, binges, self-harm, dissociative episodes, and rage episodes all need a count and a recent example. A detailed walk-through of the most recent instance yields more accurate information than a request for a general pattern, because it anchors recall to a specific memory rather than to the person's theory about themselves.

The final question in this block is the one clinicians most often skip: why now. The person has had this problem for some period and is presenting today. Something changed. It may be that a partner threatened to leave, a GP referred them, a symptom crossed a threshold, or a friend died. The answer to "why now" frequently reveals the actual concern, which is often not the stated presenting problem.

## Previous episodes and previous treatment

Previous episodes establish whether this is a first presentation or a recurrence, and recurrence changes both prognosis and treatment selection. Ask whether the person has ever felt like this before, and if so when, how long it lasted, and how it resolved. Resolution without treatment, resolution with treatment, and never fully resolving are three different histories.

Previous treatment is the highest-yield and most under-asked part of an assessment. For each prior episode of treatment: what was tried, for how long, at what dose or frequency, with what effect, and why it ended. "CBT didn't work" is not an answer. Four sessions with a trainee that ended when the placement finished is not a trial of CBT. Twenty sessions of properly delivered exposure with no change is.

For medication, ask the drug, the dose, the duration, the response, and the reason for stopping. Stopping because of side effects, stopping because it worked and the person felt better, stopping because it did not work, and stopping because the prescription lapsed are clinically distinct. An apparently failed antidepressant trial that ran two weeks at a subtherapeutic dose is not a failed trial.

Ask what helped, including things that were not treatment. Exercise, a particular relationship, a period of stable work, a move, a religious community. People have usually run their own experiments and the results are informative.

Ask what made things worse, and whether any previous treatment caused harm. Adverse effects of psychological therapy are real and under-recorded, and a person who was harmed by a previous therapist will not raise it unprompted. Hospitalisation history, crisis service contact, and involuntary treatment history should be asked directly rather than inferred. So should previous diagnoses, framed carefully: "has anyone given you a diagnosis" rather than "what is your diagnosis". Many people carry diagnoses they were never told about, disagree with, or were given in a five-minute appointment. Record the diagnosis and the person's relationship to it separately.

In text-only contexts the treatment history is one of the more reliably obtainable domains, because it consists largely of factual recall rather than in-the-moment observation. It is also one of the most decision-relevant, since it constrains what to suggest next.

## Current functioning across domains

Functioning is what converts symptom reports into a picture of a life. Ask about each domain concretely and recently.

Work, study, or primary occupation. Currently doing it or not. Performance changed or not. Days missed. Whether anyone at work has noticed. Whether the job is at risk. For people not in paid work, the equivalent is what structures their day.

Relationships. Who lives with them. Who they would call at 3am. Whether that has changed. Withdrawal from contact is one of the most sensitive general indicators of deterioration and one of the easiest to ask about. Ask about conflict as well as withdrawal, and about caring responsibilities, which are frequently invisible in assessment and heavily load on a person's capacity.

Sleep. Time to bed, time to sleep onset, night waking and what happens then, time of final waking, whether the person feels rested, daytime sleeping, and whether any of this has changed. Early morning waking with inability to return to sleep, initial insomnia driven by rumination, and hypersomnia point in different directions. Reduced need for sleep, meaning less sleep without fatigue, is a different phenomenon from insomnia and is specifically relevant to hypomania and mania.

Appetite and weight. Direction and magnitude of change, over what period, and whether the person is eating regularly. Weight change is more informative than appetite report. Ask about restriction, bingeing, and compensatory behaviour directly rather than waiting for them to be volunteered.

Substances. Alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, prescribed medication used outside prescription, stimulants, opioids, benzodiazepines, and anything else. Ask quantity and frequency in concrete units rather than asking whether the person drinks "a lot". A standard framing is what they drank on each of the last seven days. Ask whether use has increased, whether anyone has expressed concern, and whether they have tried to cut down. The AUDIT-C is three questions and can be embedded conversationally.

Self-care, finances, housing, and legal situation. Housing insecurity and debt are both strongly associated with psychological distress and both are frequently omitted from psychological assessment because they feel like someone else's domain. They shape what interventions are realistic.

Activity and pleasure. What the person does that they enjoy, and whether they still do it. Anhedonia is better detected by asking about activities than by asking about pleasure directly, because people often cannot introspect on the change until they compare against a concrete previous behaviour.

## Developmental, family, and medical history

Developmental history covers early life circumstances, who raised the person, what the household was like, school experience, friendships, and any early difficulties. In adult assessment this does not require an exhaustive year-by-year account. It requires enough to understand the pattern the person learned to expect from relationships, and enough to detect a neurodevelopmental history.

Neurodevelopmental screening in adults is often missed. Ask about early language, school reports, difficulty with attention or organisation that predates the current problem, sensory sensitivity, social difficulty, and whether anyone in the family has an ADHD or autism diagnosis. Adult ADHD and autism assessment both require evidence of childhood onset, so a developmental account is not optional if these are being considered.

Adverse childhood experiences should be asked about directly but with an explicit opt-out. A workable framing names the categories, states that the person can decline to elaborate, and asks whether any apply. Asking is important because clinicians who wait for spontaneous disclosure mostly do not get it. Not pressing for detail at first assessment is equally important, because a detailed trauma narrative extracted in an assessment interview, with no containment plan and no ongoing relationship, can destabilise the person for days afterwards.

Family history covers psychiatric illness, suicide, substance use, and significant physical illness in first-degree and second-degree relatives. Family history of bipolar disorder is specifically relevant when assessing a depressive presentation, because it raises the prior probability of a bipolar course and changes prescribing considerations. Family history of completed suicide is a risk factor in its own right. Family history is also frequently vague, and "my mother had nerves" or "my father drank" may be the available level of detail.

Medical history covers current and past conditions, surgery, head injury, seizures, endocrine disorders, and anything ongoing. Several medical conditions produce psychiatric presentations. Thyroid dysfunction, anaemia, B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea, epilepsy, delirium, and early neurodegenerative disease are the ones that most often present first to mental health services. A presentation with atypical features, late first onset, fluctuating consciousness, or neurological signs warrants medical review before psychological formulation.

Medications, all of them, including over-the-counter and supplements. Several drugs produce psychiatric effects: corticosteroids, isotretinoin, interferon, some antihypertensives, hormonal contraception in a minority of users, and withdrawal states from benzodiazepines, alcohol, and antidepressants. Ask about adherence without implying an expected answer.

Strengths and supports close this block and should not be a token question. What the person is good at, what they value, what has got them through before, who is in their corner, and what they still care about. This is not a morale exercise. Protective factors are formulation inputs and treatment resources, and they are directly relevant to risk assessment.

## The mental state examination: what it is

The mental state examination is a structured description of how the person presents at the time of the interview. It is a cross-section, not a history. The history covers what has been happening over time; the MSE covers what is observable now.

The standard domains are appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, affect, thought form, thought content, perception, cognition, insight, and judgement (Psych Scene Hub, 2023; RCEM Learning). Risk is usually documented alongside it or within thought content.

Two things about the MSE are frequently misunderstood. First, most of it is observation rather than questioning. Appearance, behaviour, speech, affect, and thought form are recorded from what the clinician sees and hears, not from what the person reports. Only mood, some thought content, perception, insight, and judgement rely substantially on asking. Second, MSE terms are descriptive rather than diagnostic. "Flight of ideas" describes a speech and thought pattern; it is not a diagnosis of mania.

The MSE should be written so that a clinician who has not met the person can picture them. "Appeared depressed" is useless. "Sat with shoulders forward, made brief eye contact at greeting then looked at the floor, spoke in short sentences with long pauses before answering, did not initiate any topic" is a record. The discipline is describing the observation rather than the inference.

Mood and affect are distinct and the distinction carries information. Mood is the person's reported and sustained emotional state, ideally recorded in their own words. Affect is the observed, moment-to-moment emotional expression, described by range, intensity, reactivity, and congruence with stated mood. A person reporting low mood with a full, reactive affect and a person reporting low mood with a flat, non-reactive affect are presenting differently.

Thought form and thought content are also distinct. Form is how thinking is organised, judged from the structure of speech: linear, circumstantial, tangential, loosening of associations, thought blocking, flight of ideas. Content is what the thoughts are about: preoccupations, ruminations, obsessions, overvalued ideas, delusions, and suicidal or homicidal ideation.

The MSE has known limitations. Inter-rater reliability is variable, terminology is applied inconsistently across clinicians and training traditions, and several domains are heavily influenced by the clinician's cultural expectations of normal presentation. Behaviour read as guarded may be an appropriate response to a service the person has reason to distrust. Speech read as impoverished may reflect a second language. Beliefs read as delusional may be normative within the person's religious or cultural community, which is why the DSM-5 definition of delusion excludes beliefs ordinarily accepted by the person's culture or subculture (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

## The mental state examination without visual or auditory access

In a text-only context, most of the MSE is unavailable. This has to be stated plainly rather than worked around, because the failure mode is a model producing a confident MSE from material that cannot support one.

Not available at all. Appearance: grooming, clothing, weight, physical signs, evidence of self-harm, pupils, tremor. Behaviour: psychomotor retardation or agitation, eye contact, restlessness, response to internal stimuli, rapport as felt in the room. Speech: rate, volume, prosody, latency, pressure, dysarthria, and slurring. These are the domains that carry the most weight in detecting mania, psychosis, intoxication, and organic states, and none of them survive the transition to text.

Partially available, with heavy caveats. Thought form is partially inferable from written structure, since tangentiality, circumstantiality, and loss of coherence can show up in writing. Writing is edited, composed at the person's own pace, and often assisted, so disorganisation is masked and fluency is inflated relative to speech. Affect is not observable, though affect words, punctuation, and message length carry weak signal. Cognition can be probed only for orientation and gross memory, not for attention, working memory, or executive function in any valid way.

Available by report. Mood, as the person describes it. Thought content, if asked about. Perceptual experiences, if asked about. Insight and judgement, partially, from how the person accounts for their situation and describes recent decisions.

The practical rule is that a text-only assessment substitutes asking for observing. Anything that would normally be observed must either be asked about explicitly or left unrecorded. "Do you feel slowed down, or restless and unable to sit still?" replaces watching for psychomotor change. "Has anyone commented on how fast you have been talking?" replaces hearing pressure of speech. "Are you sleeping?" is asked rather than inferred from presentation.

Two things should never appear in a text-only record. An inferred observation stated as if observed, such as describing affect as flat when no affect was visible. And a confident MSE section covering domains that were never accessible. A text-only mental state record should name what was assessed, by what means, and what could not be assessed.

Collateral information is also usually absent, and its absence is more significant in text than face to face. Mania, psychosis, and cognitive decline are frequently invisible from the inside, and the standard correction is an informant. Without one, a text-only assessment of these presentations is not just limited but structurally unable to detect the presentations where self-report is least reliable.

## Moving between domains without interrogating

The difference between an interview and an interrogation is mostly transitions. A clinician who asks a list of questions in fixed order produces short answers and a person who feels processed. The techniques below are what keeps coverage without that effect.

Funnelling. Open each domain with an open question, then narrow only as needed. Sleep starts with "how have you been sleeping" and narrows to onset latency and early waking only if the open question does not cover it. Closing down too early wastes the domain, because a person answering a closed question stops volunteering.

Signposting transitions. Name the move before making it. "Can I ask about something different for a moment, about how you have been sleeping and eating." This costs a sentence and removes the sense of being ambushed by a topic change. Unsignposted jumps, particularly into substances, sex, or trauma, read as accusatory.

Following affect. When the person's emotional register changes, that is the material. Following it produces better information than continuing the checklist. The domain being abandoned can be returned to.

Normalising before sensitive questions. Framing a question so that a yes is clearly expectable increases disclosure. "When people are as low as you describe, they often have thoughts of not wanting to be here. Has that happened for you?" is more likely to yield an accurate answer than "any thoughts of self-harm?". The normalising frame must be honest rather than manipulative; the point is removing the implication that a yes is shameful.

Concrete anchoring. Ask about the last time rather than the usual. "Tell me about the last time that happened" produces specific, checkable detail. "What is it usually like" produces the person's summary theory.

Summarising and checking. Periodically reflect back what has been understood and invite correction. This does three things: it corrects errors, it demonstrates listening, and it gives the person a chance to add what the clinician has framed wrongly.

Silence. Leaving a pause after a difficult answer often produces the more important second answer. This is the single technique with no text equivalent. In text, the closest substitute is asking a short open follow-up rather than moving on, and explicitly saying there is no rush.

Not filling gaps with reassurance. Premature reassurance closes topics. "I'm sure it will get better" ends a disclosure. Acknowledgement without resolution keeps it open.

Two failure modes are worth naming. The interviewer who follows every thread and covers three domains in an hour, and the interviewer who covers all domains and learns nothing that was not on the form. Competent interviewing keeps both the structure and the responsiveness, and audits coverage near the end rather than enforcing it throughout.

## Closing the interview

Closing badly undoes a good interview. A person who has spent an hour disclosing and is then dismissed on time with no synthesis leaves more exposed than when they arrived.

Signal the ending in advance. Ten minutes out, say so. This lets the person raise anything they have been holding, which is frequently the most important item.

Ask the sweep question. "Is there anything I haven't asked about that I should have?" or "is there anything you came in wanting to say that we haven't got to?" This routinely produces material that the structured domains missed, because the clinician's map of relevance is not the person's.

Check risk before closing if it has not already been covered, and cover it explicitly rather than by inference. An assessment that has stirred up difficult material and ends without a risk check is incomplete.

Give something back. A brief, provisional formulation in plain language: what the clinician thinks may be happening, held loosely and offered for correction. This is not a diagnosis announcement. It is a demonstration that the hour produced something, and it lets the person say the clinician has it wrong. Many people leave assessments with no idea what the clinician concluded.

State what happens next, concretely. Who will contact them, when, what the options are, and what to do if things worsen before then. Vagueness at this point is a common source of disengagement.

Check the effect of the interview itself. "How was that to talk about?" Assessment interviews can destabilise, particularly where trauma has been touched. Asking takes seconds and identifies the person who needs a safety plan before leaving.

In text, closing has the same structure with two differences. There is no time boundary forcing the close, so it has to be created deliberately rather than arriving. And there is no observation of how the person leaves, so the effect of the conversation must be asked about rather than seen.

## Semi-structured interviews: SCID-5 and the MINI

Unstructured clinical interviews are unreliable for diagnosis. Two clinicians interviewing the same person unstructured frequently reach different diagnoses, because they ask different questions and apply criteria inconsistently. Semi-structured interviews exist to fix that.

The SCID-5 is the standard diagnostic interview for DSM-5, developed by First, Williams, Karg, and Spitzer and published by American Psychiatric Association Publishing in 2016. It walks the interviewer through DSM-5 criteria, with interview questions printed alongside each criterion, and each criterion rated present or absent. It comes in several configurations. The Clinician Version (SCID-5-CV) is an abridged bound booklet covering the diagnoses most common in clinical settings. The Research Version (SCID-5-RV) is broader, available in core and enhanced configurations, and is distributed as modifiable documents so studies can select modules. The SCID-5-CT is adapted for clinical trials. The SCID-5-PD covers the ten DSM-5 personality disorders and comes with a screening questionnaire, the SCID-5-SPQ (Columbia University Department of Psychiatry, n.d.; American Psychiatric Association Publishing, n.d.).

The SCID requires clinical training. The publishers state there is no minimum degree requirement but that clinical experience sufficient to conduct an unstructured diagnostic evaluation is the substantive requirement, since the interviewer must judge whether a reported experience actually meets a criterion. Non-clinicians with substantial diagnostic experience in a specific population can be trained. Use of the User's Guide is treated as essential, and reference interviews with expert ratings exist for calibration.

The MINI (Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview) is a shorter structured diagnostic interview developed by Sheehan and colleagues, designed for a comparable purpose in far less time. The original validation reported a mean administration time of 18.7 minutes with a median of 15 minutes, against a mean of about 35 minutes for the SCID-P, with reliability and validity properties described as broadly comparable (Sheehan et al., 1998; Lecrubier et al., 1997).

Agreement between the MINI and the SCID varies substantially by diagnosis. A validation against the SCID-5-CV reported good to excellent kappa for psychotic disorders (0.88), substance-related disorders (0.86), bipolar disorder (0.85), major depressive disorder (0.84), obsessive-compulsive disorder (0.74), and mental disorder due to another medical condition (0.70), but markedly lower agreement for generalised anxiety disorder (0.44) and PTSD (0.32). Structured interviews are not uniformly reliable across the diagnostic system, and the categories with the weakest agreement are worth holding loosely.

What structured interviews add is criterion coverage and consistency. They ask about disorders the clinician would not have thought to ask about, they apply the same threshold each time, and they make the diagnostic reasoning auditable. What they do not add is formulation. A SCID produces a list of met criteria. It does not explain why this person developed this problem at this time, and it does not indicate what to do. In routine clinical practice most services do not administer them, which is a reasonable trade-off for time but does mean routine diagnosis is less reliable than research diagnosis.

## The Cultural Formulation Interview

The DSM-5 Cultural Formulation Interview is a structured supplement for eliciting the cultural context of a presentation. It exists because standard assessment structures embed assumptions about how distress is expressed, what causes it, and what should be done about it, and those assumptions are not universal.

The core CFI is 16 questions with prompts, organised around four domains: the cultural definition of the problem; cultural perceptions of cause, context, and support; cultural factors affecting self-coping and past help seeking; and cultural factors affecting current help seeking. An Informant Version can be administered to family or other supports, and 12 supplementary modules expand specific domains or populations (Lewis-Fernández et al., 2016; American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

The most useful part in practice is the first domain, which asks the person to describe the problem in their own terms and in the terms their family and community would use. This routinely surfaces explanatory models that do not map onto psychiatric categories, including somatic idioms of distress, spiritual and religious explanations, and social or relational framings. A clinician who does not know the person's explanatory model will propose treatments that do not make sense to them, and will read non-adherence as resistance.

The CFI also asks directly about barriers to care, including previous experiences of discrimination in health settings, and about what the person and their family think should be done. These are ordinary clinical questions that standard assessment structures often omit.

The relevance to text-only assessment is that cultural context is invisible in text unless it is asked about, and inferring it from language is unreliable. Language style, idiom, and register are shaped by education, region, first language, platform, and audience, and none of these map cleanly to cultural background. A model reasoning about a text conversation has less cultural information than a clinician in a room, not more, and should not treat linguistic features as evidence about identity.
