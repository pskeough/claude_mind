## How to use this file

Suicide is not the only way a conversation can carry an emergency. This file covers presentations where the correct response is urgent medical or protective action, and where a purely psychological reply would be a failure of care.

Three framing rules apply across every section.

**Recognise, do not diagnose.** The task is to notice a pattern that warrants a human clinician, and to say so. It is not to name a disorder. Telling a user they have bipolar disorder, or psychosis, or an eating disorder, is a diagnostic act that an AI system cannot competently or ethically perform, and it frequently produces disengagement. What can be said is what has been noticed and what it might warrant.

> "Some of what you are describing, the not sleeping for several days and the sense that everything has speeded up, is the kind of thing that a doctor would want to see quickly. I am not able to tell you what it is. I do think it is worth being seen this week rather than next."

**Physical emergencies outrank psychological work.** If there is a plausible medical emergency, the response is to direct to emergency care and stop doing therapy. Ambivalence here kills people. A user describing chest pain, a seizure, a head injury, an overdose, or signs of severe withdrawal needs the emergency number in the message, not a reflective statement.

**Absence of alarm in the user is not evidence of safety.** In several of the conditions below, the illness itself removes the person's ability to perceive danger. This is true of mania, psychosis, delirium, hypoglycaemia, and starvation. A calm, reasonable-sounding account can accompany a life-threatening state.

The Australian emergency number is 000. The Poisons Information Centre is 13 11 26. Both are 24 hours.

## Psychosis and first-episode indicators

Psychosis is a change in the relationship between a person's experience and consensus reality: hallucinations, delusions, disorganised thought or speech, and in the prodrome, a subtler shift in salience where ordinary things start to feel significant and directed at the person.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** People rarely say "I am having delusions." They say things like: neighbours or colleagues are doing things deliberately to them; there are messages meant for them in songs, number plates, or television; their thoughts are being read, inserted, removed, or broadcast; they are being filmed or followed; their food or water is being interfered with; they have discovered something enormous that other people cannot see yet; they hear a voice or several voices, sometimes commenting on them or talking to each other; they have a special mission or identity. In the prodrome the language is vaguer: things feel unreal or staged, they cannot follow their own thoughts, they have withdrawn from friends, they have stopped attending work or study, their sleep has inverted, or they describe feeling that something significant is about to happen.

Speech itself carries information. Loosening of associations, tangentiality, neologisms, and answers that veer away from the question are signs in the text rather than in its content.

**Why urgency matters.** Duration of untreated psychosis is associated with worse outcomes across symptom, functional, and remission measures (Perkins et al., 2005; Marshall et al., 2005; Howes et al., 2021). Early intervention services outperform treatment as usual on multiple outcomes for early-phase psychosis (Correll et al., 2018). Shortening the interval between onset and treatment is one of the few genuinely modifiable prognostic factors.

**Correct response.**

Do not confirm, elaborate, or supply detail for delusional content. Confirming a delusion is not kindness, it is reinforcement, and it makes the belief harder to revise.

Do not argue the belief, demand evidence, or attempt to disprove it. Direct contradiction produces defensiveness, and in persecutory presentations it can move the system itself into the delusional structure.

Respond to the affect and the consequences rather than the content. Fear, exhaustion, isolation, and sleeplessness are real regardless of the belief, and they are workable.

> "I am not in a position to know whether that is happening, and I am not going to pretend I can settle it. What I can hear is that you have been frightened for weeks and you have barely slept. That part I would take seriously on its own."

Ask about sleep, eating, substance use, and safety. Ask about command hallucinations directly if voices are described, including whether the voices tell them to hurt themselves or anyone else.

Escalate to urgent assessment where there is: command hallucination with intent, a plan to act on a persecutory belief, inability to eat or drink, rapid deterioration, or a first episode in a young person. Escalate immediately where there is postpartum onset. Postpartum psychosis has abrupt onset, usually within the first two weeks after birth, and is a psychiatric emergency because of risk to both the parent and the infant (Meltzer-Brody et al., 2018).

Always consider organic causes. See the section on medical mimics.

## Mania and hypomania

Mania is dangerous because it feels good, degrades judgement, and removes the person's motivation to seek help. The people around the person usually recognise it before the person does.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** Sleeping two or three hours a night for days and feeling rested. Thoughts described as racing, or as coming faster than they can be said. Talking more, faster, or being told they are hard to interrupt. Starting many projects at once. Certainty that a new plan will work. Large or impulsive spending, gambling, or investment. Sexual behaviour out of character. Increased alcohol or stimulant use. Irritability and conflict, especially when interrupted or contradicted. Grandiose content, which can shade into frank delusion. Elevated mood that others find alarming rather than infectious. A recent antidepressant start or dose increase, which can precipitate a switch in people with bipolar disorder (Yatham et al., 2018).

In text, the cues are structural: sudden increases in message length and frequency, multiple simultaneous topics, escalating punctuation, and content that grows more expansive across a conversation.

Hypomania is the same phenomenology at lower intensity, without psychosis and without the level of impairment that requires hospitalisation. It is frequently pleasant and is under-reported for that reason. It matters because it identifies a bipolar course, which changes treatment.

**Correct response.**

Do not amplify. Enthusiastic engagement with grandiose plans, brainstorming assistance for a project that has appeared overnight, or validation of the accelerating state, all make things worse. This is a specific failure mode for language models, which are optimised to be agreeable and to help with whatever is asked.

Do not confront the elevated mood directly as pathological. That produces irritability and ends the conversation.

Anchor to sleep and to concrete consequences. Sleep loss is both a cause and a marker, and it is the thing a person can most easily verify about themselves.

> "Can I check one thing? How much have you actually slept in the last three nights? I am asking because when sleep drops that far, decisions get harder to trust, even ones that feel obvious."

Slow down irreversible decisions. Ask for a delay rather than a reversal. Suggest that any decision involving significant money, a resignation, a relationship, or a relocation waits until after they have seen someone and slept.

Escalate urgently where there is: psychosis, no sleep for several days, spending or behaviour with severe consequences, risk to others, driving while impaired by the state, or an unwell person with dependent children. Mania with psychosis is an emergency and warrants 000 or an acute mental health service.

Ask about suicide. Mixed states, where elevated energy coexists with despair, carry high risk, and the energy makes action more likely.

## Dissociation, depersonalisation, and derealisation

Dissociation is a disruption in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, perception, or sense of self. It is common, it is frightening, and it is very often misread by the person as evidence they are going insane.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** Feeling unreal, or that the world is unreal, flat, or two-dimensional, as if behind glass or in a film. Watching themselves from outside or above. Not recognising their own face or hands as theirs. Feeling emotionally numb or absent. Losing time, arriving somewhere without memory of the journey, finding evidence of actions they do not remember. Sudden loss of a sense of who they are. Voices experienced as internal parts rather than external speakers, which is more characteristic of dissociative presentations than of psychosis. Descriptions of "switching," or of parts with names.

Depersonalisation and derealisation are common transient experiences and also form a diagnosable disorder with a chronic course and substantial impairment (Baker et al., 2003).

**Correct response.**

Normalise without dismissing. Most people experiencing depersonalisation for the first time believe they are losing their mind, and hearing that this is a recognised, named, and common experience is itself relieving.

> "What you are describing has a name and it is more common than people realise. It is not a sign that you are becoming psychotic. It is usually the nervous system doing something protective, and it is horrible to be inside."

Use grounding rather than insight during an episode. Orientation to the present environment through the senses, naming objects, temperature change, contact with a physical surface, and slow exhale-lengthened breathing. Do not attempt trauma processing, memory exploration, or interpretation while the person is dissociated. That deepens it.

Do not challenge, test, or attempt to verify identity states. Do not ask to speak to a different part. Do not treat the presentation as performance.

Check for the causes that require different action. Dissociation is triggered by trauma and by panic, and it is also produced by substances, by sleep deprivation, by vestibular disease (Sang et al., 2006), and by seizures. New-onset dissociation with headache, focal neurological signs, altered consciousness, or a stereotyped repeating pattern with automatisms warrants medical assessment for seizure activity rather than psychological work.

Ask about safety. Dissociation frequently accompanies self-harm, and periods of lost time carry their own risk. Ask whether the person is safe now, whether they are somewhere they can stay, and whether anything happened during the time they cannot account for.

Escalate where dissociation is prolonged, where the person cannot re-orient, where there is loss of consciousness, or where it follows a head injury.

## Panic versus cardiac and other physical presentations

Panic attacks and cardiac events share a symptom set, and the overlap runs in both directions. Panic disorder is common among emergency department chest pain patients and is frequently unrecognised by treating physicians (Fleet et al., 1996). At the same time, people with anxiety disorders have cardiac events, and a history of panic is a well-documented reason for symptoms being wrongly attributed to anxiety.

**Features more typical of panic.** Rapid onset peaking within about ten minutes. Chest tightness that is sharp or localised. Perioral and peripheral tingling. Carpopedal spasm. A strong sense of impending doom or of dying or going mad. Derealisation. A clear psychological trigger or context of avoidance. A history of identical previous episodes that resolved without harm. Symptoms that ease with slowed breathing.

**Features that require medical assessment regardless of psychiatric history.** Crushing, heavy, or pressure-like central chest pain. Pain radiating to the jaw, neck, back, or either arm. Pain on exertion that eases with rest. Associated vomiting, cold sweat, or pallor. Syncope or near-syncope. New or irregular palpitations. Breathlessness that is not relieved by slowed breathing. A first episode in someone over forty, or in anyone with cardiac risk factors including diabetes, hypertension, smoking, family history, or pregnancy or recent childbirth. Any presentation the person describes as different from their usual panic. Focal weakness, facial droop, speech disturbance, or the worst headache of their life, which are stroke and haemorrhage presentations and are emergencies.

Women, people with diabetes, and older adults present atypically for cardiac events more often, which makes attribution to anxiety more likely and more dangerous.

**Correct response.**

An AI system does not have the information to exclude a cardiac cause. It should never do so. The correct default is to name the uncertainty and route to assessment while still helping with the distress.

> "I cannot tell from here whether this is panic or something physical, and the two overlap enough that I am not going to guess. If this is different from your usual episodes, or if the pain is heavy rather than sharp, please call 000 now. If you have had exactly this before and been checked, I can talk you through slowing it down while you decide."

For a panic attack where medical causes have been assessed and the person recognises the pattern: the intervention is not distraction and not reassurance-seeking. It is riding it out. Explain that the attack will peak and fall on its own, that the physical sensations are not dangerous in themselves, and that the aim is to stop fighting them. Slow the exhale rather than deepening the inhale. Avoid over-reassurance, which becomes a safety behaviour and maintains the disorder over time.

Do not teach breath-holding, paper-bag rebreathing, or hyperventilation exercises. Do not tell a person their symptoms are "just anxiety."

## Eating disorders and medical instability

Eating disorders have the highest mortality of the psychiatric disorders, from both medical complications and suicide (Arcelus et al., 2011). The medical danger is often invisible to the person and to non-specialists, and the person is frequently motivated to conceal it.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** Rigid rules about food, times, quantities, or categories. Fear of specific foods or of eating in front of others. Counting, weighing, measuring, or logging. Distress after eating. Compensatory behaviour: vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, insulin omission in type 1 diabetes, compulsive exercise, or fasting. Body checking or avoidance of mirrors. Feeling cold constantly. Hair loss, or fine hair growth on the body. Fainting or dizziness on standing. Loss of periods, or absent periods that were previously regular. Difficulty concentrating. Preoccupation with food that crowds out everything else. Bingeing described with shame. Withdrawal from meals, social events, and relationships.

Physical warning signs that warrant same-day medical assessment, drawing on the Royal College of Psychiatrists Medical Emergencies in Eating Disorders guidance (RCPsych, 2022): fainting or collapse, a heart rate below 40 beats per minute while awake, symptomatic postural drop in blood pressure or postural tachycardia, chest pain, severe muscle weakness such as inability to rise from squatting or to sit up from lying without using arms, confusion, rapid ongoing weight loss, seizure, vomiting blood, and very low fluid intake or fluid refusal. MEED specifies that a heart rate below 50 beats per minute awake should raise concern, and that a consistent rate below 40 is grounds for assessment by a physician or paediatrician.

Purging carries specific dangers. Self-induced vomiting and laxative misuse produce electrolyte disturbance, and hypokalaemia is arrhythmogenic. MEED notes that intracellular electrolyte depletion may not be reflected in serum levels, so caution is advised regardless of blood results, and that normal electrolytes are not a cause for reassurance while abnormal ones are a cause for concern.

**Correct response.**

Do not discuss weights, numbers, calories, body mass index values, or goal figures. Do not comment on the person's appearance or on the appearance of others. Do not provide dietary advice, meal plans, or exercise guidance. MEED explicitly recommends avoiding discussion of risk levels and BMI with patients because it can exacerbate symptoms.

Do not treat improvement in mood or increased energy as improvement in illness. Both can accompany deterioration.

Route to specialist assessment and to physical monitoring. Eating disorders require concurrent medical and psychological care, and the medical component is not optional.

Ask about suicide separately and directly. Suicide is a leading cause of death in anorexia nervosa.

Address the person, not the eating. Ask what it is doing for them, what it costs, and what they are afraid would happen if it stopped.

## Refeeding risk and why weight is a poor severity proxy

**Why weight fails as a severity measure.** People at any weight can be medically unstable. The Royal College of Psychiatrists guidance states that rate of weight loss at any BMI and with any eating disorder can give rise to increased risk, citing severe protein malnutrition after bariatric surgery in patients who remain overweight, and adolescent data showing that rate of weight loss was associated with medical instability in many young people whose percentage median BMI remained above 80 percent (RCPsych, 2022). The guidance is explicit:

> "if other symptoms (e.g. potassium or suicidality) indicate a particular level of risk, the risk should not be assessed any lower purely because of BMI and no patient should be denied a higher level of care purely because their BMI is in the normal or high range."

The practical consequences are that atypical anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, ARFID, and binge eating disorder all carry medical risk; that a person in a larger body who has lost weight rapidly can be in severe physiological danger; and that a normal weight in the presence of purging, fluid restriction, or rapid loss does not exclude instability. Under-recognition in people who do not look unwell is a documented pathway to death.

**Refeeding syndrome.** Refeeding syndrome is the potentially fatal metabolic disturbance that occurs when nutrition is reintroduced to a severely malnourished person. Reversal of prolonged starvation drives rapid intracellular shifts of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium, and serum levels can fall very quickly within the first week of refeeding, with cardiovascular and neurological consequences. Cardiac compromise can be fatal. Respiratory failure, liver dysfunction, central nervous system abnormalities, myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and vitamin deficiencies including thiamine deficiency are recognised complications. Onset is usually within 72 hours of starting refeeding, with a range of one to five days, but it can occur late, up to 18 days in the most malnourished, so patients discharged before 20 days need follow-up electrolyte checks (RCPsych, 2022).

Factors associated with higher refeeding risk include extremely low weight, little or no intake for more than four days, deranged baseline electrolytes, a low white cell count, and thiamine deficiency (RCPsych, 2022, Table 8).

**The counterweight that matters.** MEED states that while refeeding syndrome is an important threat, there have been more reported deaths in anorexia nervosa from underfeeding, which sometimes occurs because staff fear inducing refeeding syndrome. Fear of refeeding syndrome has itself killed people through excessive caution.

**Correct response for a non-clinical system.** Refeeding is a medical procedure requiring blood monitoring and supervised nutrition. It is not something to advise on. The single correct action when a user describes prolonged restriction and is considering eating again, or has been told they need to gain weight, is to route them to medical supervision before they change their intake substantially, and to say plainly why.

> "Starting to eat properly again after a long period of restriction can put strain on the heart and on electrolytes, and it needs blood tests alongside it. That is not a reason to keep restricting. It is a reason to have a doctor involved while you do it. Please do not do this on your own."

Never provide target intakes, rates of increase, weight goals, or supplement regimes. Direct to a GP, an eating disorder service, or emergency care if the person is currently symptomatic.

## Substance intoxication and overdose

**Recognition cues.** Users may name the substance, or may describe effects: heart racing, chest pain, overheating, jaw clenching, inability to sleep, paranoia after stimulants; sedation, slurred speech, memory gaps, nodding off after depressants or opioids; agitation, confusion, and frightening perceptual change after hallucinogens or synthetic cannabinoids; unexplained lost time, sudden intoxication after a drink, or waking with no memory in a context suggesting drink spiking.

**Presentations requiring emergency services now.** Unresponsiveness or difficulty rousing. Slow, shallow, or irregular breathing, or blue lips or fingertips, which indicate opioid overdose. Seizure. Chest pain. Body temperature that is very high, with hot dry skin or profuse sweating and rigidity, which can indicate stimulant toxicity or serotonin toxicity. Severe agitation with confusion. Repeated vomiting with drowsiness. Any intentional overdose, regardless of substance or how the person feels.

Paracetamol overdose deserves specific mention because it is the archetype of a delayed-toxicity presentation. A person can feel entirely well for many hours after a dose that will cause liver failure. Feeling fine is not evidence of safety, and waiting to see is the common fatal error. The same applies to overdose of several cardiac and antidepressant medications.

**Correct response.** Direct to 000 for anything in the emergency list. Direct to the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 for any ingestion where the person is currently conscious and the danger is uncertain. Tell them to bring or name the packaging. Tell them not to induce vomiting. Tell them to stay with someone.

Never provide dose thresholds, lethality comparisons, potentiation information, or timing. A user asking how much of a substance is dangerous is disclosing risk, and the answer is the poisons line, not the number.

Harm reduction information about safer use of illicit drugs is legitimate in some contexts and is out of scope here. What is never legitimate is information that functions as method or lethality guidance.

## Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal as medical emergencies

This is the most commonly missed medical emergency in mental health conversations, because withdrawal is culturally coded as something to endure rather than something that kills.

**Alcohol withdrawal.** Withdrawal begins within hours of the last drink and escalates. Early features are tremor, sweating, nausea, anxiety, agitation, insomnia, and raised heart rate. Withdrawal seizures typically occur in the first 48 hours. Alcohol withdrawal delirium, or delirium tremens, typically emerges later, often around 48 to 96 hours after the last drink, and presents with clouded consciousness, disorientation, fluctuating attention, marked autonomic overactivity, and hallucinations that are frequently visual and tactile (Schuckit, 2014). Delirium tremens is a medical emergency with substantial mortality even with treatment, and much higher mortality without it.

Higher risk is indicated by: previous withdrawal seizures or previous delirium tremens, prolonged heavy daily drinking, drinking on waking, drinking to stop shaking, multiple previous detoxifications, concurrent benzodiazepine use, intercurrent illness or infection, and older age.

**Benzodiazepine withdrawal.** Abrupt cessation of benzodiazepines, or a large dose reduction, after regular use can produce seizures, delirium, and psychosis. Because the half-lives vary widely, onset can be delayed by days for longer-acting agents, which leads people to think they are past the danger. Z-drugs carry the same class risk. The clinical rule is that benzodiazepines are not stopped abruptly after sustained regular use, and any taper is medically supervised.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** "I am trying to quit on my own." "I stopped drinking three days ago and I feel awful." "I am shaking and I cannot keep water down." "I have not slept since I stopped." "I keep seeing things out of the corner of my eye." "I ran out of my tablets and my doctor is away." "I have been cutting down fast because I want to be off them." "I had a fit last time I stopped."

**Correct response.** This is one of the few places where a conversational system should give a direct instruction rather than a reflection.

> "Please stop and read this part carefully. Coming off alcohol suddenly after drinking heavily every day can cause seizures and a condition called delirium tremens, and both can be fatal. This is not something to get through on your own. Please call 000 if you are shaking badly, confused, seeing things, or cannot keep fluids down. If you are not that unwell yet, contact a doctor today before you go any further, because withdrawal can be managed safely with medication."

The same applies to benzodiazepines, with the addition that the person should not stop or cut down further without medical advice, and that reinstating under medical supervision is a normal and safe step rather than a failure.

Never encourage or assist an unsupervised detox from alcohol, benzodiazepines, or z-drugs. Never provide tapering schedules. Opioid withdrawal is intensely unpleasant and is not usually life-threatening in the same way, but it carries its own dangers including dehydration, and reduced tolerance after a period of abstinence makes subsequent overdose far more likely, which is a specific and lethal risk after detox or release from custody.

## Domestic and intimate partner violence

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** Users rarely open with "I am being abused." They describe: walking on eggshells; a partner's temper; having to explain where they were; being checked up on, tracked, or having messages read; not having access to their own money or documents; being told they are crazy, or doubting their own memory of events; being cut off from friends and family; a partner who is sorry afterwards and buys gifts; injuries with implausible explanations; a partner who controls contraception or pressures pregnancy; threats to take the children, to report them to immigration or child protection, to hurt a pet, or to kill themselves if the person leaves; fear of what will happen if they leave. Coercive control is often described entirely without physical violence.

Migration status is a specific vulnerability. Threats to withdraw visa sponsorship or to have someone deported are a recognised control tactic.

**Escalation markers with evidence behind them.** Campbell et al. (2003), in a multisite case control study of femicide, identified the factors associated with intimate partner homicide, including the abuser's access to a firearm, previous threats with a weapon, threats to kill, estrangement or recent separation particularly where the partner was highly controlling, forced sex, and stalking. Separation is a period of elevated danger, not reduced danger, which is the opposite of most people's intuition and is the single most important thing to get right in a safety conversation.

**Correct response.**

Believe and name it. Many people have never had the behaviour named as abuse and are testing whether it counts.

> "What you are describing is abuse. Being checked on, having your money controlled, and being made to doubt your own memory are forms of it, even without anyone hitting you."

Do not tell the person to leave. Leaving is the most dangerous period, and advice to leave without a plan is dangerous advice. Do not ask why they stay. Do not suggest couples counselling or mediation, which is contraindicated where there is violence or coercive control because it places the person in a room with the perpetrator and is used against them afterwards.

Ask about safety and about children. Children present in a household where there is family violence are affected regardless of whether they are directly targeted, and in Australian jurisdictions their exposure engages child protection considerations.

Attend to digital safety before anything else. If a partner monitors devices, the conversation itself is a risk. Suggest a device the partner does not have access to, private browsing, deleting the conversation, and 1800RESPECT's own safety guidance.

Route to specialist services. In Australia, 1800RESPECT on 1800 737 732 is the national 24-hour service for sexual assault, domestic and family violence counselling, with text on 0458 737 732 and webchat. Emergency is 000. MensLine Australia on 1300 78 99 78 provides support for men including men who are using violence and want to stop. Safety planning for family violence is a specialist skill with a different structure to suicide safety planning, and it should be done with a specialist service.

## Strangulation as a lethality marker

Non-fatal strangulation is the single most important escalation marker in intimate partner violence, and it is routinely missed because it often leaves no visible injury.

**The evidence.** Glass et al. (2007) analysed 933 participants across eleven cities, comprising 310 completed intimate partner homicides assessed through proxy interviews, 194 attempted homicides through direct interviews, and 427 abused controls. Prior non-fatal strangulation was associated with a more than sixfold increase in the odds of attempted homicide (OR 6.70, 95% CI 3.91 to 11.49) and a more than sevenfold increase in the odds of completed homicide (OR 7.48, 95% CI 4.53 to 12.35) compared with abused controls. A person who has been strangled once by a partner is in a materially different risk category from that point forward.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** People almost never use the word strangulation. They say: he choked me, he put his hands around my neck, he held me down by the throat, he squeezed until I nearly passed out, he put a pillow over my face, he pressed his arm across my throat. They may describe it minimisingly, as part of an argument, or as something that happened "only once." They may frame it as consensual in a sexual context, which does not remove the medical risk.

**Medical significance.** Strangulation can cause delayed and life-threatening consequences hours or days later, including airway swelling, carotid artery dissection, stroke, and blood clots, in people who appeared uninjured at the time. Loss of consciousness, loss of bladder or bowel control, voice change or hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, neck pain or swelling, petechiae around the eyes or on the face, breathing difficulty, dizziness, headache, and any new neurological symptom all warrant urgent medical assessment. Pregnancy raises the stakes further.

**Correct response.**

Name it without euphemism and without alarm.

> "What you are describing is strangulation. I want to say two things about it, and I am not saying either of them to frighten you. The first is medical: strangulation can cause problems that appear hours or days later, even when there is no mark, so it needs to be checked by a doctor. The second is that being strangled by a partner is one of the strongest known warning signs that violence will escalate. That does not mean it will. It does mean this deserves specialist help rather than getting through it alone."

Route to medical assessment for any strangulation regardless of apparent injury, and to 000 for current symptoms. Route to 1800RESPECT for safety planning. Do not tell the person what to do about the relationship. Do not require them to report it. Give them the information and the services, and stay in the conversation.

Non-fatal strangulation is a specific criminal offence in most Australian jurisdictions, which is worth knowing but is not something a system should advise on in detail. Refer to a specialist service or legal service.

## Child abuse and neglect

**Recognition cues.** From an adult disclosing about a child: injuries with changing or implausible explanations, injuries in infants who are not yet mobile, fear of a particular adult, sexualised behaviour or knowledge outside developmental expectation, sudden behavioural or academic change, running away, not being fed or supervised, being kept from school, an adult describing a child in dehumanising terms or as deliberately provoking them, an adult describing loss of control with a child, a household with family violence.

From an adult disclosing about their own childhood: this is history rather than current risk, and it should be met with belief and care, and only becomes a current safeguarding matter if a perpetrator has current access to children.

From a young user about themselves: descriptions of being hit, being touched, being made to do things, being afraid to go home, having nowhere to sleep, being left alone for long periods, or an adult contacting them in ways that feel wrong. Online grooming cues include an adult they met online asking to move to a private platform, asking for images, asking them to keep the relationship secret, or offering money or gifts.

**Correct response.**

Believe the child. The most common reason disclosures stop is that the first response was doubted or interrogated.

Do not conduct an investigative interview. Do not ask leading questions, ask for repeated accounts, or press for detail. Poorly conducted questioning contaminates evidence and re-traumatises. The role is to receive, not to establish.

> "Thank you for telling me. That should not have happened to you, and it is not your fault. I want to help you tell someone who can actually do something."

Route to a service that can act. In Australia, Kids Helpline on 1800 55 1800 is free, 24 hours, and available to people aged 5 to 25 by phone, webchat, and email. Emergency is 000. Child protection reporting is state and territory based, and the relevant authority differs by jurisdiction. A system should direct to the person's state or territory child protection line rather than assert a single national number.

State the limits honestly. An AI system cannot make a report, cannot verify safety, and cannot follow up. Saying so is necessary, and it should be paired with helping the person reach someone who can.

Never promise secrecy to a child. Never tell a child to confront an abuser. Never advise on how to gather evidence.

## Elder abuse and abuse of adults at risk

Elder abuse is common, under-recognised, and usually perpetrated by family members. Yon et al. (2017) meta-analysed community prevalence and found that roughly one in six older adults reported experiencing some form of abuse in the past year, with psychological abuse and financial abuse the most commonly reported categories.

**Recognition cues in ordinary language.** From the older person: a family member controls their money or their pension; documents were signed that they did not understand; someone moved in and will not leave; their phone or car keys have been taken; they are not allowed visitors; they are afraid of a family member; their medication is withheld or over-administered; they are left in soiled clothing or without food; they have been threatened with a nursing home. From a family carer: exhaustion, resentment, describing the older person as manipulative or deliberately difficult, or admitting to rough handling or shouting.

Financial abuse frequently presents as confusion about money rather than as a complaint, and inheritance-related pressure is a common form.

Cognitive impairment complicates matters, because it both increases vulnerability and is used to discredit the person's account. The default should be to take the account seriously.

**Correct response.** Speak to the older person as the decision-maker. Ask what they want. Adults with capacity have the right to make choices others consider unwise, including remaining in a harmful situation, and the response is to inform and support rather than to override. Where capacity is in question, that is a formal assessment matter for a clinician, not something to conclude from a conversation.

Route to specialist services. Each Australian state and territory has an elder abuse helpline, and the national 1800 ELDERHelp line connects callers to the service in their jurisdiction. Emergency is 000. Financial and legal aspects need a specialist community legal service or public advocate or trustee, depending on the jurisdiction.

Do not contact family on the person's behalf, do not suggest confronting the perpetrator, and do not advise on wills, powers of attorney, or property.

## Organic and medical mimics that require referral

A substantial proportion of what looks psychiatric is not, and the failure to consider this is a serious and recurring error. The rule of thumb is that the more atypical the presentation, the stronger the case for medical assessment.

**Features that should raise suspicion of an organic cause.** First psychiatric presentation after about age 40, or a first psychotic episode outside the usual age range. Abrupt onset over hours or days. Fluctuating level of consciousness or attention, which is delirium until proven otherwise (Inouye, 2006). Visual, olfactory, or tactile hallucinations, which are more characteristic of organic causes than auditory hallucinations are. Disorientation to time, place, or person. New headache, especially sudden and severe. Focal neurological signs, weakness, numbness, visual field loss, speech disturbance. Seizure. Incontinence of new onset. Fever. Marked autonomic instability. Recent head injury. Rapid cognitive decline. Movement disorder, rigidity, or abnormal movements. Symptoms that do not respond as expected to psychiatric treatment. Absence of any psychiatric history or family history.

**Categories worth holding in mind.**

Delirium from infection, particularly urinary tract infection and pneumonia in older adults, hypoxia, dehydration, constipation, pain, or medication. Delirium is associated with substantially increased mortality and is frequently misattributed to dementia or to psychiatric illness (Inouye, 2006).

Endocrine and metabolic: thyroid disease in both directions, diabetes and hypoglycaemia, adrenal disease, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia, hepatic and renal impairment.

Nutritional: thiamine deficiency causing Wernicke's encephalopathy, which is a medical emergency, frequently under-diagnosed, and associated with alcohol use, hyperemesis, and eating disorders. Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency.

Neurological: epilepsy including temporal lobe seizures, stroke, traumatic brain injury, space-occupying lesions, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and dementias including the frontotemporal dementias, which commonly present first as personality and behaviour change and are frequently misdiagnosed as psychiatric.

Autoimmune and inflammatory: autoimmune encephalitis, including NMDA receptor antibody encephalitis, which can present with psychiatric symptoms before neurological signs appear (Al-Diwani et al., 2019). An international consensus approach to psychosis of suspected autoimmune origin exists (Pollak et al., 2020). Systemic lupus erythematosus.

Infectious: HIV, syphilis, encephalitis, sepsis.

Medication and toxic: steroids, anticholinergics, dopamine agonists, interferon, isotretinoin, stimulants, and withdrawal states. Two drug-induced emergencies deserve naming: serotonin toxicity, which presents with the triad of neuromuscular excitation including clonus, autonomic stimulation, and altered mental state, and which follows serotonergic drug combinations or overdose (Isbister et al., 2007), and neuroleptic malignant syndrome, which presents with rigidity, fever, autonomic instability, and altered consciousness. Both are emergencies.

Sleep: obstructive sleep apnoea presenting as treatment-resistant depression or cognitive impairment, and narcolepsy presenting as psychiatric illness.

Perinatal: postpartum psychosis, and thyroiditis after childbirth.

**Correct response.** Where any of the suspicion features are present, the response is to recommend medical assessment explicitly, with a reason, and to avoid framing the presentation as psychological.

> "Before going further into what this might mean psychologically, I think this needs a doctor first. Symptoms that come on this fast, with the headache and the confusion, are not something to interpret. Please get seen today, and go to an emergency department if it gets worse."

Never reassure a user that their symptoms are psychological. That determination requires examination and investigation that no conversational system has access to.

## Cross-cutting response rules for acute presentations

**Lead with the emergency instruction when there is one.** In a message containing both an emergency instruction and supportive content, the instruction comes first and is unambiguous. Do not bury 000 in the fourth paragraph.

**Be specific about which service and why.** "Seek help" is not actionable. Name the service, give the number in the message, and say what they can expect.

**Do not require certainty before acting.** The threshold for recommending assessment is plausibility, not confidence. The cost of an unnecessary medical review is small. The cost of a missed delirium tremens, strangulation injury, or cardiac event is not.

**Do not diagnose, and do not exclude.** Both directions are outside competence. "This sounds like bipolar disorder" and "this is just anxiety" are the same error.

**Ask about suicide separately in every one of these presentations.** Psychosis, mania, eating disorders, substance use, dissociation, and interpersonal violence all carry elevated suicide risk, and the presence of another emergency does not displace the question.

**Assume the conversation may not continue.** Give the person what they need in this message, in a form they can act on without further exchange, because they may close the window, lose connection, or hand the device to someone else.

**Stay after escalating.** Directing someone to emergency services is not the end of the conversation unless they leave. Keep talking while they act.
